# Chapter 5: The Great War

The first ray of sunlight touched the sand before the northern gate, and the war drums began.

They were sorcery-enhanced, the low, pulsing rhythm of Humman war drums resonating across the desert like a heartbeat. The sound vibrated through the stone of the gate, through the bones of every Wengari standing on the wall, through the still morning air that had been silent moments before. Irek felt it in his chest. He was young, barely twenty, his golden mane still short from the Motted Paw training that had reshaped the Bright Paw champions after the tournament.

He had never fought in a war. He had never seen a scorpion charge. He had never heard the drums.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<span class="speech">"The vanguard,"</span> said the veteran beside him. Her name was Toren, a Stripe Paw with scars across her muzzle and a missing ear. She had fought in three border wars and a dozen skirmishes against the Fire Beetles. She was not afraid. <span class="speech">"Two hundred riders. They're testing our defenses. The first wave is always a test."</span>
</div>

The rune cannons fired.

They were mounted on the northern wall, twelve great constructs of rune-glass and ancient stone, their barrels aimed at the advancing line of scorpion riders. Nefere's sorcerers had been working since dawn, calibrating the runic sequences, adjusting for wind and distance and the particular imperfections of weapons that had been designed three thousand years ago and never truly tested in war. The first shot missed.

The second shot missed. The third, fourth, and fifth shots scattered across the desert, throwing up great gouts of sand that the scorpion riders simply swerved around.

Then the sixth cannon fired, and the seventh, and the eighth.

Three shots struck true. The runic energy erupted in cones of white-hot destruction, vaporizing scorpion and rider alike, carving great furrows through the Humman vanguard that closed like wounds in the sand. When the light faded, the first wave was gone. Two hundred scorpion riders, reduced to ash and memory in the space of a heartbeat. The drums faltered, then resumed, louder and faster than before.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">Irek let out a breath he had not known he was holding. "Is it over?"</p>
</div>

<div class="dialogue-block">
<span class="speech">"It's never over,"</span> Toren said. <span class="speech">"That was the test. Now comes the assault."</span>
</div>

The second vanguard was already advancing. More riders this time—two hundred and fifty, their scorpions' iridescent shells gleaming in the rising sun, their claws leaving deep furrows in the sand. And behind them, the Amuks were beginning to move, their great eight-legged forms swaying with each ponderous step, their tusks catching the light like polished ivory.

The crimson hawks were released. Forty of them, their red plumage blazing against the pale morning sky, their wings beating with the swift, steady rhythm of Steadfast-attuned predators. They flew in tight formation, their aura adaptations already humming with stored energy, and when they reached the advancing vanguard, they fired. Their feathers detached from their bodies in explosive volleys, each one a small, brilliant detonation of crimson light.

The first volley tore through the scorpion riders' front ranks, shattering carapaces and throwing riders from their mounts. The second volley struck the flank. The third, fourth, and fifth scattered the formation entirely.

But the scorpions kept coming. The hawks wheeled and fired their final volleys—their feathers spent, their bodies too drained to remain aloft—and then they glided back toward the capital, their mission complete, their numbers diminished. Irek counted thirty-two returning. Eight had fallen.

The killer claws were released next. They came from the pens beneath the eastern wall, thirty of them, their sleek raptor bodies moving at subsonic speeds across the sand. Each one was strapped with a rune bomb, a package of compressed sorcerous destruction that would detonate on impact. They did not waver. They did not slow. They simply ran, their aura-enhanced speed carrying them into the heart of the scattered vanguard, and then they exploded.

The rune bombs erupted in white-hot cones of fire and force, and the second vanguard was decimated. The survivors—perhaps fifty riders—stumbled through the smoke and carnage, their scorpions wounded and confused, their formation shattered.

But they did not retreat. The war drums were still pounding, and the Amuks were still advancing, and the third wave was already forming on the horizon.

Sera's elite unit stood at the forefront. Twenty Wengari warriors, their dark pelts gleaming in the morning light, their lacquered claws catching the sun. They had been training for this moment since the tournament, since the offices were established, since the Blight's shadow first fell across the desert. They had something to lose. Parents with cubs. Mates who had bonded. Warriors who had fought beside the same comrades for years.

They did not break formation. They did not chase glory. They simply held.

The remnants of the second vanguard crashed against the elite unit like a wave against a cliff. The scorpions were larger than any fire foot, their pincers capable of snapping a Wengari in half, their venom potent enough to kill in seconds. Irek watched from the wall as a scorpion seized a Bright Paw warrior in its claws and crushed him. He watched as another Wengari—a Shadow Paw, her dark pelt matted with blood—was thrown from her feet and trampled beneath the creature's great legs.

He watched as the elite unit closed ranks, their Eight Points training taking over, their claws finding the gaps in the scorpions' armor, their bodies moving with the cold, precise efficiency of warriors who had been forged for this exact moment.

The battle was brutal. It was not glorious. It was blood and sand and the screams of the dying, and when the last of the second vanguard fell, the elite unit stood over the bodies of their enemies and roared. Not a roar of victory. A roar of defiance. A roar that said: we are still here.

Not one of the elite unit had died. Four were wounded—one grievously, her arm hanging limp and useless at her side—but all twenty still stood. Sera walked among them, her ruined face impassive, her lacquered claws dripping with scorpion blood. She did not speak. She did not need to.

The drums paused.

Irek felt the silence like a physical weight. The sun was higher now, climbing toward mid-morning, and the desert was littered with the corpses of scorpions and riders and the smoldering remains of rune bomb detonations. The first wave had been a test. The second had been an assault. And the third wave—four hundred riders, the full strength of the Humman army—was pressing forward.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">The Amuks were still advancing. The Tide Wolf cavalry was still in reserve. And the Wengari had not yet won. Irek tightened his grip on his spear and looked at Toren, who was still standing beside him on the wall, her scarred face unreadable. "What happens now?" he asked.</p>
</div>

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">Toren did not look at him. "Now we find out if we are strong enough."</p>

The war drums resumed. The third wave began to move—four hundred scorpion riders, their iridescent shells a sea of shifting colours in the climbing sun. The Amuks were still advancing, the Tide Wolf cavalry circling wide. On the wall, Irek tightened his grip on his spear and watched the tide roll toward the gate. The elite unit below stood silent, bloodied but unbowed, their dark eyes fixed on the approaching storm.

It was the seventh day of the Month of Storms, in the first year of the reign of Ajani Brightmane, first of his name. The White Dawn lay unconscious in his chambers, the golden armor still pulsing faintly over his chest, Black Fire and Red Fire curled protectively on either side. The regent stood in the war room, her silver aura flickering like a guttering candle. And the great war had only just begun.

At 5:25 in the morning, Vasha stormed into the war room unannounced.

Sylva stood at the head of the granite table, her silver-chased ceremonial armor still gleaming from the dawn light that streamed through the high windows, but everything else about her was fraying. Her ancient eyes were hollow from three weeks without proper sleep. Her silver aura flickered erratically, no longer the calm, patient pulse of Flowing Water but something jagged, something struggling to maintain its rhythm.

She had not eaten since the previous evening. She had not rested since the water hole. And the battle was only minutes old.

Sylva felt the vibration in her chest, in her bones, in the ancient roots of the palace that had stood for three thousand years. Dust sifted from the ceiling. The oil lamp on the table flickered and steadied.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">Silence. A long, agonizing silence, broken only by the distant pounding of the Humman war drums. Then the scout returned, breathless, his golden eyes wide. <div class="dialogue-block">

The crimson hawks were aloft—forty of them, their red plumage blazing against the pale morning sky, their aura adaptations humming with stored energy. She could not see them from the war room, but she could imagine them: the explosive volleys of their feathers, the detonations ripping through the scorpion ranks, the riders falling, the scorpions screaming.

Nefere opened her clouded eyes and stared at the window, where the sun was climbing steadily toward mid-morning, and she said nothing at all.

<div class="dialogue-block">

The door to the war room slammed open. Vasha stood in the doorway, her humorless face even more humorless than usual, her striped mane bristling with the particular fury of someone who had been running through the palace corridors for the past ten minutes. She was not supposed to be here. She was supposed to be in the council chamber, managing the Untrustworthy. But Vasha had never been good at staying where she was supposed to be.

<div class="dialogue-block">

Then she said, in a voice that was utterly without inflection, <span class="speech">"The third wave is four hundred strong. The elite unit is twenty. The guards on the wall are a hundred. If the gate falls, the city falls. And the king is still unconscious."</span> She paused. <span class="speech">"I thought you should know."</span>

Sylva stared at her. The war drums pounded on, low and patient and utterly without fear. The third wave was advancing. The Amuks were coming. And the great war was only twenty-five minutes old.



The Hall of the Untrustworthy was a small, windowless chamber beneath the Great Hall, its walls lined with rune-glass filaments that pulsed with soft amber light. The table at its center was round. The five members sat in their accustomed places, their faces lit from below by the amber glow, their shadows stretching long and distorted across the stone walls.

The war drums had not yet begun. The first rays of dawn had not yet touched the sand. But the council had known for three weeks that the Humman army was coming, and they had not been idle.

He was not a spy. He was not a diplomat. He was a merchant who had drawn the short straw.

The war drums pounded outside, low and relentless, and the tent was filled with the smell of sweat and incense and the particular metallic tang of sorcery-enhanced communications equipment.

Outside, the third wave was forming—four hundred scorpion riders, the last of the vanguard, the last hope of a king who had never fought a battle. The Amuks were still advancing, their great tusked forms swaying with each ponderous step. The Tide Wolf cavalry was still circling to the east. And the Wengari capital still stood, its walls unbroken, its gate unbreached, its king not dead but sleeping in a chamber high above the battle, waiting for the moment when he would wake.

At 6:55, Sera held the gate.

Sera stood at the gate, her ruined face impassive, her lacquered claws dripping with the blood of the second vanguard. Around her, the elite unit was still breathing hard, their dark pelts matted with sweat and sand and the ichor of scorpions. Four of them were wounded—one grievously, her arm hanging limp—but all twenty still stood. They had held. They had won.

And then she saw the third wave.

Four hundred scorpion riders. The entire remaining vanguard of the Humman army, stretching across the horizon in a sea of iridescent shells and gleaming claws. Behind them, the Amuks were forming a line, their great tusked forms swaying in unison, their eight-legged strides eating up the distance between the desert and the gate. The Tide Wolf cavalry was circling to the east, fifty massive wolves with riders in striped armor, waiting for the moment when the gate would fall and the slaughter would begin.

'We can't win against four hundred. We barely survived fifty.'

The thought was cold and clear and utterly without panic. Sera had been an assassin for decades. She had learned to count enemies before she learned to read. She knew when a battle was winnable and when it was not. This battle was not winnable. Not with twenty warriors. Not with the cannons still recharging. Not with the hawks and the killer claws already spent.

The Bright Paw guards on the wall loosed a volley of arrows down into the advancing scorpions, but the shafts bounced harmlessly off their iridescent shells.
