## Chapter 3: 06:25 — The War Room Still Watches

It was 6:25 in the morning, the seventh day of the Month of Storms, and the war room was thick with exhaustion and fear. Sylva stood at the head of the granite table, her silver-chased ceremonial armor still gleaming, but everything else about her was fraying. The rune cannons had fallen silent. The wall was burning. The third wave was pressing against the gate. And Vasha had just stormed through the door.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"I knew you old fools would still be here. I bet you don't even know why the Hummans are here, right?"</p>
</div>

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">Sylva looked at her. The regent's ancient eyes were hollow, her silver aura flickering with the guttering light of a candle in a storm. She had not slept in three weeks. She had not rested since the water hole. She had held the capital together with patience and will and the quiet, inexorable force of Flowing Water, and she was running out of all three. "And you do? Please. Enlighten us."</p>
</div>

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">Vasha stepped forward. Her humorless face was even more humorless than usual, and there was something in her cold eyes that Sylva had never seen before. Not satisfaction—Vasha was not capable of satisfaction. Something closer to vindication. "You should thank the wisdom of our young king. And you should renounce your post, Sylva. It was your maid. How could you not notice?"</p>
</div>

She threw a sheaf of papers onto the granite table. They scattered across the maps—copies of correspondence, written in the precise, careful hand of a Humman scribe. A map of the capital, its gates and walls annotated in meticulous detail. Guard rotations. Patrol schedules. The times of day when the watch changed and the wall was most vulnerable. And a letter, dated three weeks ago, addressed to His Wealthiness Mekhmed the Third of Verdantis.

The White Dawn is dead. He fell into a coma after some Wengari ritual. The city is in chaos. The elders are divided. The army is scattered. If you march now, you will find the gate unguarded and the capital defenseless. Come quickly. I will leave this channel open.

Sylva's silver aura flickered. Her ancient eyes scanned the documents once, twice, three times. The handwriting was unmistakable. She had seen it every day for months—on trade manifests, on council summaries, on the endless scrolls of governance that crossed her desk. Lena. Seris's daughter. The frightened Humman scribe who had been assigned to her service as surety for her people's good behavior. The girl who scrubbed stone channels and carried water and learned to be still in the gardens while Sylva taught her patience.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">Her aura guttered. Her face went pale beneath its silver fur. "I—she was—I did not—"</p>
</div>

<div class="dialogue-block">
<span class="speech">"You did not notice."</span> Vasha's voice was flat, uninflected, utterly without mercy. <span class="speech">"She was your maid. She sat outside your chambers every day. She copied your correspondence. She knew everything. And you did not notice."</span>
</div>

The elders stared at Sylva. Zara's golden eyes were wide with shock. Kareth's scarred face was unreadable, but his one good eye was fixed on the regent with an intensity that bordered on accusation. Nefere's clouded gaze was distant, her ancient mind already calculating the implications. The Bright Paw elders—those who remained—were looking at each other with dawning horror.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">Sylva's legs gave way. She did not fall—she was too old, too disciplined, too deeply trained in Flowing Water to fall—but she sank onto the edge of the granite table, her silver aura flickering like a dying star. "She was so quiet. She never complained. She learned to scrub the stone channels. She learned to sit in the garden. I thought—I thought I was teaching her patience. I thought she was learning."</p>
</div>

<div class="dialogue-block">
<span class="speech">"She was learning,"</span> Vasha said. <span class="speech">"She was learning everything. And then she used it. Your maid almost destroyed the capital. The king's intelligence network saved it. Remember that, when you write your resignation."</span>
</div>

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">The Shadow Paw general stepped forward. His name was Torin—no relation to the Motted Paw champion—a veteran of the border wars whose dark pelt was cross-hatched with scars. His voice was calm, cold, the voice of a warrior making a tactical assessment. "I am relieving the regent of her command. Effective immediately. The chain of command is broken. The wall is burning. We need soldiers on the gate, not politicians in the war room. Send the Pyrinae sorcerers to the wall. Now."</p>
</div>

No one protested. Sylva did not protest. She simply sat on the edge of the table, her silver aura still flickering, her ancient eyes fixed on the papers scattered across the maps.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">Vasha looked at her for a long moment. Then she spoke, and her voice was very slightly less cold. "Don't worry. Our council will save you. We sent for reinforcements. Woh riders from the east and west. Black flame Tide Wolves from the south. Ghosts from the Rune-Belt. They will arrive. You just need to endure." She turned and walked toward the door, her striped mane bristling, her humorless face set in its usual expression of cold calculation. The war drums pounded on outside, and the wall was still burning, and the regent of the Wengari sat alone in the war room, her silver aura guttering in the darkness.</p>
</div>

Nefere stood on the eastern post of the northern wall, her ancient frame silhouetted against the burning sky. The fireballs from the Amuk artillery had stopped falling—the Hummans were repositioning, preparing for the final assault that would breach the gate—but the wall was still smoking, and the bodies of the fallen still lay where they had been struck. Below her, the scorpions were massing again. Three hundred and fifty of them, their iridescent shells catching the climbing sun, their claws clicking against the stone in a restless, hungry rhythm.

The third wave was about to crash against the gate.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">She raised her rune-glass mirror. It was a small thing, no larger than her palm, its surface shimmering with a pale, silver light. It had been passed from High Speaker to High Speaker since the time of the Third Tyrant, entrusted to each new bearer with the same words: "You will never use this unless the capital itself is in danger. If that day comes, you will know what to do." Nefere had carried it for forty years, since the day her predecessor pressed it into her young paws and told her to keep it close. She had never used it. She had hoped she would never need to use it. She spoke into the mirror, her ancient voice calm and steady despite the chaos around her. "Is everyone in position?"</p>
</div>

<div class="dialogue-block">
A crackle of static. Then a voice—a young Pyrinae sorcerer, her words clipped and urgent. <span class="speech">"South front in position."</span> Another voice, older, rougher. <span class="speech">"North front in position."</span> A third, barely more than a whisper. <span class="speech">"West front in position."</span>
</div>

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">Nefere closed her eyes. The four cardinal points of the capital, each anchored by a Pyrinae sorcerer with a mirror like hers, each one waiting for the signal that would either save the city or doom it. She planted her rune-glass staff on the stone and spoke a single word. "Begin chanting."</p>
</div>

The rune formation began to appear over the city. It started as a faint shimmer in the air above the palace, a ring of pale golden light that pulsed once, twice, and then steadied into a slow, rotating circle. A second ring appeared below it, smaller, spinning in the opposite direction. Then a third. Then a fourth. The rings began to rotate faster, their light intensifying, and the Wengari below—the soldiers on the walls, the wounded in the courtyards, the terrified civilians huddled in the Bright Paw temple—stared up at the sky with expressions of wonder and dread.

Solen recognized it. The old High Priest had been kneeling in the temple sanctuary, his golden mane still dusty from the frantic ride back from the water hole, his ancient heart still heavy with the guilt of the armor. He had looked up when the first ring appeared, and he had known immediately what it was. The light shield. He had seen it on the temple walls, carved into the ancient reliefs that depicted the Lightbringer's greatest works—the forging of the pact, the building of the hanging gardens, the raising of the capital from the desert stone.

It was ancient sorcery, older than the Pyrinae, older than the Wengari, a gift from the lord of the desert in the days when the pact was new and the Golden Sun still walked among them. He whispered the name under his breath, and the Wengari around him took up the whisper, and the whisper became a murmur that spread through the temple like ripples in still water.

The golden dome bathed the city. It descended from the rotating rings like a curtain of light, a hemisphere of hard, shimmering gold that enclosed the capital from the northern gate to the southern wall, from the eastern barracks to the western gardens. The scorpions on the wall screeched and fell back, their claws smoking where the light touched them. The Amuk artillery fired again, and the fireballs crashed against the dome and scattered into nothing.

The Wengari inside the city cheered—a ragged, desperate roar of relief that echoed through the streets and courtyards and up to the burning walls.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">Nefere did not cheer. She stood on the eastern post, her rune-glass mirror still pressed to her ear, her clouded eyes fixed on the rotating rings above her. The light shield was beautiful. It was also fragile. It was also temporary. She raised the mirror again. "How long?"</p>
</div>

<div class="dialogue-block">
A crackle of static. Then the voice of the southern sorcerer, quieter now, almost apologetic. <span class="speech">"One hour. Perhaps an hour and a half, if the wind holds and the sun stays high. And then—"</span> A pause. <span class="speech">"And then never. The mirrors will burn out. The sequence cannot be repeated. We have one hour, High Speaker. One hour to hold the city. After that, the light shield will fall, and it will never rise again."</span>
</div>

Nefere lowered the mirror. The golden dome shimmered above her, beautiful and doomed, and the scorpions were still massing at the gate. One hour. Perhaps an hour and a half. The reinforcements were coming, but they were still out in the desert, still riding, still flying, still surging toward the capital with all the speed they could muster. One hour. She had to hold for one hour. She planted her staff on the stone and began to chant.

The Humman command tent was chaos. Mekhmed the Third was on his feet, his silk robes billowing, his jeweled fingers pointing at the golden dome that had just swallowed the Wengari capital whole. The light shield shimmered in the morning sun, beautiful and terrible, and every scorpion that had been climbing the wall was now retreating across the sand, their claws smoking, their riders cursing.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<span class="speech">"WHAT IS THAT?! SPEAK! WHAT IS THAT? WHY DID NO ONE TELL ME THEY HAD THAT?!"</span> Mekhmed's voice cracked. He turned on his generals, his face flushed with fury and something that looked very much like fear. <span class="speech">"We were almost there! The gate was breaking! The wall was burning! And now—that! What is that?!"</span>
</div>

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">His generals stood in their loose semicircle, their faces carefully neutral. General Tamsin, her scarred face still set in the cold mask of someone who had been ordered to shut it and had not forgotten, was the first to speak. "We don't know, Your Wealthiness. The Wengari have not used this defense in living memory. The Pyrinae sorcerers must have been keeping it in reserve. But it is clearly a defensive barrier—hard light, ancient sorcery, probably temporary. We should take advantage of the pause to retrieve our wounded. The scorpions that fell from the wall may still be alive. The riders who were burned may still be saved. We have a window. We should use it."</p>
</div>

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">Mekhmed stared at her. The golden light of the dome reflected in his dark eyes, and for a moment, something flickered across his face—uncertainty, perhaps, or the first faint stirrings of the realization that he had marched his army nine hundred kilometers into a trap he did not understand. Then it was gone, replaced by the petulant fury of a king who had never been told no. "Fine. Whatever. Bring back the gear and the surviving scorpions. I paid for those scorpions. I paid for that gear. I did not pay for it to be abandoned in the sand."</p>
</div>

The generals shot him a look. It was not a look of respect. It was not a look of obedience. It was a look of pure, unadulterated hatred—the hatred of soldiers who had been ordered to fight a war they did not believe in by a king who did not understand what he was asking. Tamsin held his gaze for a long, cold moment. Then she turned and walked out of the tent without speaking, her scarred face set, her dark eyes burning. The other generals followed, their silence more damning than any protest.

Outside, the golden dome still shimmered over the capital. The scorpion riders were retreating across the sand, dragging their wounded behind them, their iridescent shells scorched and smoking. The Amuks stood motionless, their great tusked forms silhouetted against the climbing sun. And the Humman army, which had been moments from victory, was now waiting—waiting for orders, waiting for the light shield to fall, waiting for a king who did not know how to lead them.

The generals were oath-bound. They would obey. They would retrieve the wounded. They would salvage the gear. They would prepare for the next assault. But they would not forget. They would not forgive. And they would not die for a king who valued scorpions more than soldiers. The war drums pounded on, low and patient and utterly without loyalty. The light shield shimmered over the capital. And the great war was not yet over.

The dome shook. Every volley from the Amuk artillery sent ripples of golden light cascading across its surface, and the sound—a deep, resonant boom like a great bell being struck—reverberated through the streets and courtyards of the capital. The common Wengari had taken refuge wherever they could. The eastern market, where Ajani had lifted Kira by the paw and run from the guards, was empty now, its stalls abandoned, its fire feet pens silent.

The western barracks were full of wounded soldiers, their golden and striped and shadowed pelts matted with blood and sand. The streets were a chaos of shouting and weeping and the endless, pounding rhythm of the war drums outside the gate.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">A Bright Paw priest stood at the entrance to the temple, his golden mane singed from a fireball that had struck the outer courtyard, his ceremonial robes stained with the blood of the wounded he had carried inside. He raised his voice above the din, shouting into the chaos. "ALL TO THE TEMPLE! WE HAVE HEALERS! FRESH WATER! FOLLOW MY VOICE!"</p>
</div>

The common Wengari came. They streamed through the temple gates—Stripe Paw merchants who had been trapped in the capital when the army arrived, Bright Paw servants who had never held a weapon, Shadow Paw elders who were too old to fight but still sharp enough to pray. The priests moved among them, distributing water and bandages and the quiet, steady reassurance of ritual. The temple walls were covered in the ancient reliefs of the Lightbringer's deeds, and the golden light of the dome outside filtered through the high windows, casting long, distorted shadows across the stone.

In the royal chamber, the world was quieter. The dome's booms were distant here, muffled by the thick stone walls of the palace. Lira stood guard outside the door, her lacquered claws gleaming in the dim light, her dark eyes fixed on the corridor. She had been appointed after the second assassination attempt, and she had not left her post since. Vex stood across from her, his dark pelt blending with the shadows, his silence a familiar, steady presence.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">Lira spoke without looking at him. "Shouldn't we be out there? On the wall? The city is burning."</p>
</div>

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">Vex did not look at her either. "Our duty is to the king. And his sister. We do not move."</p>
</div>

Lira's claws tightened. She had been humiliated at the water hole, presented as a bride and dismissed as a misunderstanding. She had been given a second chance guarding Kira, and she had taken it without hesitation. But she could hear the dome shaking. She could hear the distant screams. She could hear the war drums. And she was standing in a quiet corridor while the city burned. Vex said nothing. His silence was answer enough.

Inside the chamber, Kira was curled in the corner. Her dark pelt was matted with sweat, her small body trembling. The white scorpion—Pearl, she had named her Pearl—was pressed against her chest, her pale antennae waving anxiously. Black Fire and Red Fire had left their posts on Ajani's bed and crawled toward her, their dark and crimson carapaces glowing faintly with the residual light of the golden armor.

They did not understand what was wrong. They only knew that their imprinted master was unconscious in the bed behind them, and his sister was crying in the corner, and they did not know how to fix either of these things.

The dome boomed. Kira flinched.

She was six years old again. She was standing in the rubble of the west wall, her dark fur caked with dust, her small paws bleeding from the broken stone. The Wallbreaker beetle was roaring somewhere in the distance, and her mother's hands—her mother's strong, calloused armorsmith's hands—were pressing a crust of bread into her paws. "Stay here. Stay hidden. Don't make a sound. I'll be back. I'll be back."

Her mother had not come back. Her father had not come back. The wall had collapsed, and the beetles had come through, and she had hidden in the rubble for two days before anyone found her. She had been six years old. She had been alone.

The dome boomed again. Kira sobbed.

The scorpions hummed. It was the same low, rhythmic vibration they had used when Ajani first held them at the water hole—the not-quite-purr that had calmed him when he was afraid. Black Fire pressed his dark head against her knee. Red Fire wrapped her crimson tail gently around her ankle. Pearl, the smallest, the fiercest, the one who had deflected an assassin's blade, crawled into Kira's lap and pressed her iridescent shell against her chest.

They hummed together, the three of them, a tiny chorus of comfort in the dim light of the royal chamber. And Kira, who had been alone for three years, who had found a brother and then lost him to a coma and a war and a city under siege, buried her face in Pearl's pale shell and wept.
