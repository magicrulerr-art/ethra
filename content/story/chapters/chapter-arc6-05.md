## Chapter 5: Epilogue

Nikolai switched to a wide sweep, the halberd's blade cutting a glittering arc through the morning air. Ajani jumped, the halberd passing beneath him, and the Tsar's stern face split in a fierce grin. He had been testing the boy. The boy was good. He thrust upward, changing direction with the speed of a warrior who had been fighting for longer than most Wengari had been alive. But Ajani was already summoning his staff—the grandfather, the fourth pillar—and he used it to parry the halberd's upward thrust.

The force of the parry drove the halberd down, its blade biting into the sand, and by the time Nikolai lifted it, Ajani was already descending. The ji materialized in his paws—the weapon of the Deep, the blade of green fire, the form he had not yet mastered but had learned to trust. He landed on the shaft of the halberd, his weight pinning it to the ground, and the tip of the ji came to rest gently against Nikolai's throat.



The Tsar of the Snow Paws looked up at the White Dawn. For a long moment, no one moved. On the snow wurm, Anastasia watched with her quiet, frightening stillness, her pale blue eyes taking in every detail of the exchange. Vanya's hand had moved to her axe, but she had not drawn it. Nadya was frozen, her daggers forgotten in her fingers. Ivan's mouth was hanging open. Cefiro simply nodded, a single, deliberate inclination of his head, as if to say: *I told you.* In the shadow of the gate, L'vat let out a long, rattling breath — annoyance, or pride.

The Veylar queen watched in silence from her coral palanquin, her ancient eyes unreadable. The Wengari on the wall—M'rak, Reva, Zephyr, Yvaria, Sylva—stood ready, their claws half-extended, unsure whether to intervene or applaud.

The scorpions clicked their pincers in perfect unison—a sound of recognition, of welcome, of joy.


Kira didn't wait for them to reach her. She launched herself off the wurm's back, her dark fur streaming behind her, and landed between the two scorpions with the practiced ease of someone who had been doing this since the day she met them. Blackie's antennae waved frantically over her head, checking for injuries. Reddy's tail curled around her shoulders in a protective arc.

The city woke in darkness. The first grey light of dawn had not yet touched the eastern horizon when the great bronze doors of the palace swung open. Ajani Brightmane walked through them, and he was wearing black. The robe was simple, unadorned, the color of ash and mourning. The diadem on his brow was the only thing that gleamed, its white gem pulsing with a slow, steady light. Behind him, Blackie and Reddy walked in perfect silence, their white-lacquered pincers dimmed, their tails lowered.

They understood. Even the scorpions understood.


The Bright Paw guards came next, forty of them, each one carrying a coffin on their shoulders. The coffins were simple—wood from the Rune-Belt, polished smooth, unadorned except for the names carved into their lids. Wengari names. Forty-seven of them. Behind the Bright Paws, the Pyrinae carried their own dead—twelve sorcerers who had died in the mirror array detonation and the artillery exchanges. Behind the Pyrinae, the Hummans carried their fallen—the civilians who had grabbed table legs and scythes and marched to the wall, the scorpion riders who had defected and fought alongside the Wengari.

The procession walked through the silent streets of Styxian, and wherever it passed, the city joined it.


Wengari stepped out of their homes in black robes. Hummans emerged from the Humman quarter in dark tunics. Pyrinae engineers and Stripe Paw merchants and Shadow Paw assassins and Motted Paw observers fell into step behind the coffins. No one spoke. The only sound was the slow, rhythmic tread of paws on stone, and the distant beat of the ghosts' drums high above the city—a mourning rhythm, slow and steady, the sound of grief translated into bone-vibration and wingbeat.


Nikolai walked beside Ajani, his massive frame draped in a black robe that the servants had worked through the night to prepare. He had not known the fallen. He had not fought in the war. But he was Wengari, and the Wengari honored their dead. On Ajani's left walked the Veylar queen, her iridescent skin dimmed to a deep, mournful blue, her ancient eyes fixed forward. Her Shell-Singers walked behind her, their Petal-Shells pulsing with a soft, rhythmic light that matched the drums.


The procession reached the northern gate. The coffins were laid in a long line across the blackened sand, where the Plague creature had nearly breached the wall, where the Wohs had died, where the ghosts had fallen. The bodies of the Wengari and the Pyrinae and the Hummans were placed side by side, without distinction. In death, they were all the same.


Ajani turned to face the crowd. The black robe billowed around him in the cold dawn wind. The diadem's gem blazed. When he spoke, his voice carried across the silent desert, and every ear heard him.


<div class="dialogue-block">
<span class="speech">"Life in the desert is hard. Only those strong enough survive. The weak die. It has always been this way. It will always be this way. From the sky, dozens of lighted arrows fell. They streaked through the dawn like falling stars, trailing arcs of golden fire. They struck the coffins in a single, simultaneous impact, and the pyre erupted. The flames rose against the pale morning sky, golden and orange and red, consuming the dead, carrying their spirits home.


Ajani did not turn. He stood before the fire, the heat washing over him, the light of the pyre reflecting in the diadem's gem. Blackie and Reddy flanked him, their tails raised in salute. The Wengari watched in silence. The Hummans watched in silence. The Pyrinae watched in silence. The Snow Paws and the Veylar and the Threx watched in silence. The fire burned. The dawn came. And the dead were honored.





The procession returned to the city in silence. The sun was fully risen now, and the streets that had been empty in the darkness were still empty in the light. Ajani had declared the day a mourning day. No work. No practice. No duties. Only silence. Only remembrance. The Wengari understood. The Hummans understood. Even the Quick, who did not fully grasp the concept of death, understood that this was a day for quiet.


At the eastern plaza, the memorial pillar stood alone. It was a simple column of white stone, quarried from the deep veins beneath the Pyrinae district, its surface polished to a mirror sheen. The names of the fallen had been carved into every face of the pillar—forty-seven Wengari, twelve Pyrinae, the Hummans who had died on the wall. The names wrapped around the stone in a spiral, rising toward the sky.


Ajani stopped before the pillar. The black robe billowed around him in the cold morning wind. He raised one paw and extended a single claw. He drew it across the pad of his finger, and a thin line of blood welled up against the golden fur. He reached out and drew the blood across the stone—a single, crimson line that marked the pillar at the height of his heart. Then he stepped back and walked toward the palace. He did not speak. He did not need to.


One by one, the city followed. Nikolai drew his own blood and marked the stone, his massive frame dwarfing the pillar. Anastasia followed, her quiet stillness unchanged, her blood as red as any Wengari's. The Veylar queen extended one webbed hand, and a single drop of iridescent blood fell onto the stone, shimmering for a moment before sinking into the white surface. L'vat drew no blood—the Threx did not bleed as other races did—but he touched one limb to the pillar, and a faint green luminescence lingered where his chitin had pressed.



M'rak and Reva and Zephyr and Yvaria marked the stone. Sera marked the stone. Sylva marked the stone. The Pyrinae engineers who had rebuilt the wall marked the stone. The Stripe Paw masons who had hauled stone alongside the king marked the stone. The Bright Paw guards who had carried the coffins marked the stone. The human refugees who had fought on the wall marked the stone. Seris marked the stone, and when her blood touched the pillar, her dark eyes glistened for just a moment before she moved on.



Kira marked the stone last. She was the smallest, and she had to stand on her toes to reach the height where the others had left their marks. Her blood was as red as Ajani's. She pressed her finger to the pillar and drew a line beside her brother's. Then she stepped back and looked at the pillar—white stone, black names, red lines, the blood of every race that had fought for the city mingling together on the same surface. Pearl clicked her pincers once. Remembered.


The city returned to silence. The day passed. And the memorial stood in the eastern plaza, marked by the living, waiting for the next dawn.



The atrium was a thing of beauty, a temporary structure of white stone and golden rune-glass that the Pyrinae had raised in a single night. Its columns were carved with the sigils of the four families, and its domed ceiling was open to the sky, the morning sun pouring down in shafts of amber light. The banners of every allied race hung from the rafters—the golden sunburst of the Wengari, the crystalline sigil of the Pyrinae, the coiled scorpion of the Hummans, the tidal spiral of the Veylar, the mycelial web of the Threx, and now, for the first time, the frozen star of the Snow Paws.



Ajani sat on the throne in full royal regalia, the white spear of the Pyrinae humming in his paw. The diadem's gem blazed on his brow. The bracers gleamed on his forearms. The armor had shifted again, adapting to the formality of the occasion—not battle mode, but something regal, something that spoke of ceremony rather than war.


To his right, Blackie and Reddy flanked the throne. The scorpions were wearing bespoke capes of deep crimson, embroidered with gold thread by the palace tailors. The capes bore intricate motifs—Blackie's depicted him dissolving the assassin who had tried to kill Kira, his tail raised, his green venom streaming. Reddy's depicted her beside him, her pincers raised in the victory dance she had performed on the wall after Blackie dissolved the falling stone.

The scorpions held perfectly still, their white-lacquered pincers gleaming, their tails arched in regal posture.


To Ajani's left, Kira sat on a small throne that had been placed specifically for her. She was wearing a miniature set of lacquered armor that the Shadow Paw smiths had crafted over the past week—dark plates that gleamed with a subtle iridescence, fitted perfectly to her small frame. Pearl was perched on her shoulder, her pale carapace polished to a mirror shine, her antennae waving with quiet dignity.

Kira's dark eyes swept across the assembled crowd with the composed assessment of someone who had learned to read a room before she learned to read.


Beside her sat Nikolai, his massive frame draped in the formal robes of the Snow Paw court—white furs trimmed with silver, the frozen star sigil embroidered on his chest.
