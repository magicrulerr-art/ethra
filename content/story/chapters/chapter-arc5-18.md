The light wall cracked. A fissure ran across its golden surface like lightning across glass, and the creature on the other side screamed in triumph. Its chitinous claws hammered against the barrier, each blow sending fresh fractures spider-webbing through the ancient radiance. The Golden Cloaks braced themselves, their light pillars flickering, their golden armor blazing with the last reserves of their power.

But the creature had been pounding against the wall for four minutes, and the wall was breaking. With a sound like the shattering of a world, the light wall fell. The golden radiance dissolved into a shower of dying sparks, and the creature stepped through, its purple eyes blazing, its eight legs churning the sand.

The purple orbs where its eyes should have been flickered and dimmed. The creature collapsed, prostrate on the sand, its two halves barely connected by a thin bridge of black ichor. And then the mirror array exploded. The ancient crystals, already cracked and straining, finally shattered under the load. The tower blew apart in a shower of golden sparks and molten sand. The beam died. The Hydromancers were thrown from their posts, their rune-glass staves dark, their bodies limp. Nefere was hurled backward, her ancient frame crashing against the stone. The mirror array was gone. It would never fire again.</p>

The purple orbs flickered back to life, brighter than before, angrier than before. The creature rose, its eight legs steady beneath it now, its movements smooth and controlled. It had been hurt, truly hurt, and it had learned.

It was 11:59 in the morning, the seventh day of the Month of Storms, in the first year of the reign of Ajani Brightmane. Noon was one minute away. The creature was rising. The Golden Cloaks were preparing their final stand. And the order to evacuate the capital was about to be given.

Ajani's eyes opened.

The shockwave that burst from the royal chamber was not a blast of force or fire. It was subtler than that—a tremor of light that rippled outward from the palace like a stone dropped into still water. It passed through the stone walls without cracking them, through the temple without shaking its foundations, through the shattered rampart without dislodging a single stone. But every living thing in the capital felt it.

In the war room, Sylva stopped mid-stride and turned toward the palace, her silver aura flickering with something that was not exhaustion. In the temple, the wounded looked up from their pallets, and the children stopped crying. On the wall, M'rak felt it pass through his chest like a heartbeat that was not his own, and his burn-scarred arms broke out in gooseflesh.

The creature felt it too. It faltered. Its eight legs, which had been carrying it toward the gate with increasing steadiness, stumbled. The purple orbs where its eyes should have been flickered, and its massive head swiveled toward the palace. It let out a sound that was not a scream but something lower, something that might have been uncertainty. Then it began to move faster, its legs churning the sand, its body lurching toward the wall with a new urgency.

It knew what had happened. It knew what was coming.

In the royal chamber, Kira was on her feet. The cocoon of light that had enclosed her brother was dissolving, the pearlescent radiance fading into the golden armor beneath. Black Fire and Red Fire were humming, their small bodies vibrating with a frequency that matched the fading light. Pearl was on Kira's shoulder, her pale antennae lifted toward Ajani. Cefiro stood with his back against the stone wall, his pale blue eyes wide, his notebook forgotten in his paw.

The golden armor was glowing, but it was no longer the cold, desperate light of a relic trying to wake its dead master. It was warm. It was steady. It was alive.

Ajani sat up. The crown of white Styx feathers still encircled his head, its flames no longer flickering with the wild instability of the coma but burning steady and bright. The breastplate of pearly scorpion chitin still molded to his chest. The bracers of fire beetle chitin still clasped his forearms. The white spear that had been the Pyrinae's gift and the armor's transformation still lay beside the bed.

He looked at Kira, his green-flecked eyes clear and aware. He looked at Cefiro, the white Wengari he had not seen in three years, and a flicker of recognition crossed his face. He looked at the scorpions, still humming, still pressed against his legs. And then he rose. It was noon. The White Dawn had woken.

Ajani's eyes adjusted to the light. The golden glow of the armor had faded to a steady, warm radiance, and the cocoon that had enclosed him was dissolving into faint motes of pearlescent light that drifted upward like snow in reverse. He was lying on his own bed, in his own chamber, but something was different. The air smelled of ancient magic and the faint, lingering sweetness of the Plague mist. The distant sounds of battle—the screams, the cannon fire, the pounding of the creature's claws—had not stopped.

He had been hearing them in his sleep, woven into the dark dreams of the coma, and now he heard them clearly. He sat up.

His body felt strange—lighter than it should, as if the armor were carrying some of his weight. The white Styx crown was still on his head, its flames steady and bright. The breastplate still molded to his chest. Black Fire and Red Fire were pressed against his legs, their small bodies vibrating with a low, urgent hum. Pearl was on the bed beside him, her pale antennae waving. Kira was staring at him from the corner, her dark eyes wide, her mouth open, her small body frozen mid-step as if she had been about to run to him and had forgotten how.

And Cefiro was standing against the far wall.

The white Wengari was exactly as Ajani remembered him—tall, pale-furred, his body pelted with black motes, his pale blue eyes watching with an expression of patient calm. He was holding a small, leather-bound notebook in one paw, and he looked exactly as he had three years ago in the great city of ice, when Ajani had stumbled half-frozen into the Snow Paw court and asked if he could rest by their fire.

It was noon, seventh day of the Month of Storms, first year of the reign of Ajani Brightmane.

Ajani's head snapped toward the window so fast Kira heard the bones in his neck crack. He did not seem to notice. He was on his feet before she could speak, before Cefiro could finish his sentence, before the scorpions could untangle themselves from his legs. He crossed the chamber in three strides and threw open the balcony doors.

The smell hit him first. Blood and smoke and the faint, lingering sweetness of the Plague mist. The sounds came next—the screams, the cannons, the pounding of the creature's claws against the desert floor. And then the sight. The wall was broken. The eastern section was gone, carved away by the mirror array's beam. The northern approach was a wasteland of blackened sand and purple mist and the bodies of the fallen.

Wohs lay dissolving in pools of green goo. Ghosts lay where they had fallen, their five-meter wingspans crumpled and still. And the creature—the black thing, the nightmare given form—was advancing on the gate. Sixteen meters of chitinous black flesh and eight legs and two purple Plague orbs for eyes, and it was one league away.

Ajani did not speak. He reached into the air and the grimoire was there—the Motted Paw gift, the living book, still tucked into the satchel he had carried since the water hole. He opened it to a blank page and began to write. The formula flowed from his claws in the precise, elegant script L'vat had drilled into him: air, lift, speed, duration. The concentric circles of a self-contained flight sequence. He ripped the page from the book and pressed it against his own chest.

<p class="speech-line">Golden wings erupted from his back. They were translucent, shimmering, the color of the Steadfast at noon, and they spread wide enough to catch the desert wind. He did not look back. He simply stepped off the balcony and flew toward the wall. Cefiro watched him go. His notebook was still on the floor, his pale blue eyes were still wide, and his voice, when he finally found it, was thoughtful and slightly bemused. "Well. That is new."</p>

They enveloped Kira and the scorpions, Cefiro and the unconscious forms of Vex and Lira, lifting them gently from the stone floor. The bubbles rose and drifted toward the balcony, and then they were outside, following the golden trail of Ajani's wings toward the shattered wall. It was noon. The White Dawn was awake. And the creature was still coming.</p>
