## Chapter 3

**A Cub's Play**

The royal families had gone down into the ice together — Tsar and Tsarina, the king among them — and the palace had felt their going the way a hearth feels a door left open.

Sylva had stayed behind. The cavern was their matter. The city was hers — and the largest single piece of the city's business just now was the festival, born in the Council of Coin and so far made entirely of announcements and arithmetic.

She went down to find the two people who could tell her how much of that arithmetic had become coin: the Shadow Paw who kept the ledgers, and the ambassador whose Sultan had promised to pay for everything.

The Hall of the Untrustworthy lay beneath the Great Hall — a small, windowless chamber, a single round table lit from below by rune-glass filaments pulsing soft amber, so that every face at it glowed upward and every shadow ran long and crooked up the stone.

She heard the argument before she reached the door.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"We can do it on the fourth moon."</p>
</div>

Vasha's voice came through the stone flat and unhurried, without a single inflection in it — the voice of a ledger being read aloud. She sat at the head of the round table, as she had sat at the head of it on the council's first day, because she had arrived first and claimed the seat, and no one had ever been foolish enough to contest it.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"We already have the logistics for the big tournament. A small festival is a cub's play."</p>
</div>

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"No."</p>
</div>

The word came from behind a rampart of scrolls, old and papery and immovable. Toren did not look up from his writing — he had been volunteered onto this council because no one else had wanted the seat, and he had served it ever since the way he served everything: thoroughly, and against his better judgment.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"At least a month. Let our spies vet every delegation before one of them sets a paw inside the walls."</p>
</div>

Maren huffed.

The Shadow Paw coin master leaned back across the table, lacquered claws tapping the stone once — the tap of someone who has heard a worry she finds personally insulting.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"We are ready today. Let them come and try something."</p>
</div>

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"After the war, we deployed our members here. No one lifts a claw in this city without us hearing it."</p>
</div>

At the far end of the table, Sylen — Elyra's apprentice, young for a seat at this table — wrote it all down and said nothing, which was, so far, his entire contribution to the council, and a wiser one than most.

And on the far side, as far from Vasha as the table allowed, Kellan the Silk-Weaver sat with his hands flat and still on the stone. The trembling had left him sometime after the reparations were signed. The distance had not.

Sylva stood in the doorway and let the argument wash past her.

Nobody stopped. The Council of the Untrustworthy did not perform for regents — it had been built in a war to do its work whether anyone watched or not. Only Maren marked her: one flick of the dark eyes toward the door, weighed, catalogued, filed away, and back to the fight in the space of a breath.

Inside, the argument was not really about a festival. It was about the war, still — about which of them the city needed most against the world. The timetable. The caution. The listening.

And in the middle of it, while Stripe Paw and Bright Paw fought the fourth moon up and down the table, Maren turned her head — unhurried, as if the matter were already settled, because to her it was — to the Humman ambassador at her elbow, and asked the only question in the room that had nothing to do with time.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"So — how many carts of coin do we need, Seris?"</p>
</div>

Seris did not answer at once. The ambassador looked into the middle distance with the expression of a merchant pricing a thing that had not yet been built.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"Hmm. Perhaps fifteen. Eighteen."</p>
</div>

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"It will depend how many delegations we get. From Verdantis — and from the Veylar."</p>
</div>

Sylva listened to it all from the doorway — the moons and the month, the claws and the carts — and interjected.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"Ambassador Seris, may I have a word?"</p>
</div>

**The Missing**

The chamber's argument had not stopped — Stripe Paw and Bright Paw still traded fourth moons and whole months up and down the amber-lit round table — but Sylva had drawn the ambassador a little apart from it, into the margin of the room where the rune-glow ran thin and the shadows went long and crooked up the stone.
Seris came willingly. She had been waiting, perhaps, for someone to speak to her as a mother rather than a creditor.
Sylva did not offer comfort. The Grand Vizier was not built for it. She delivered what she had the way she delivered everything — plainly, and all at once.
<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"We found someone who saw your daughter leave from the east gate while the battle was going on."</p>
</div>
Something in the ambassador's face reached for light then — a hope so quick and so desperate it was painful to watch it catch.
<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"And we also found some hummans who said they saw a woman with a similar description at the human camp."</p>
</div>
Sylva paused. It was the pause of a Wengari counting what remained before she gave it all away.
<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"Regrettably, we did not find her among the dead. That is all I have for you now. I would not hold out hope — there are still many hummans missing from when the creature attacked."</p>
</div>
The words settled into the quiet between them. Seris let a drowned breath out of her — the breath of a swimmer who has surfaced for just long enough to see that the sea is still all around.
<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"Thank you, Grand Vizier. I know my daughter brought a calamity at your gates, and you had no reason to honor my request for finding her. I will not forget this."</p>
</div>
She said it like a woman carving a debt into stone with the point of a knife. Grief had not made her smaller. It had made her still — the way pressure makes a thing hard.
<div class="thought-block">
*Verdantis did this to her. I will burn that city to the ground for what they did to my Lena. Just you wait.*
</div>
The thought did not cross her face. It had no need to. It crouched in her, patient as a drawn blade; and for a single breath there was no diplomat in the room, only a mother, and a promise.
Then a voice cut across the argument at the table, unconcerned with either grief or courtesy.
<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"Hey! Grand Vizier! When are we going to start the festival? Has his Highness decided yet?"</p>
</div>
Vasha of the Stripe Paw had not even risen from her head seat. She shouted from it — loud, at ease, the voice of a Wengari who owned the room and knew it. Sylva turned to her, composed and unhurried, the whole weight of the palace settling back onto her shoulders.
<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"Tomorrow, if he had his way. It seems the creature from the marshes wants to finish his training — something his Highness is very keen to avoid. He is taking our royal cousins for a tour of the city, I believe. I saw him leave with his pets a while ago."</p>
</div>
Royal cousins.
Vasha's ears came forward at that, sharper than any question she had asked. The Snow Paws — long-lost kin, returned from the north out of exile. The four great families called each other brothers, and always would. But the Snow Paws were not brothers. They were cousins: family, truly, by blood and by story, and yet set apart by a hundred generations of ice, so that the word carried affection without quite carrying intimacy. Kin you loved and did not quite know. And the king had taken them walking.
<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"Is that so…?"</p>
</div>
Vasha's smile came slow, and behind it, in her flat, cold eyes, a glint — not amusement. Appraisal. The look of a Wengari weighing a thing she had not yet decided to spend.
The information sat on the table between them, unclaimed, and she handled it the way Maren handled a ledger or a trader handled an unguarded caravan: as currency. The festival. The king's refusal of his training. The creature from the marshes, growling for its pupil in the dark. The cousins, walking where the king walked.
The fourth moon. The month. Tomorrow. Vasha did the arithmetic in her head, and her smile got a degree warmer, and nothing in the room was quite as safe as it had been a moment before.
