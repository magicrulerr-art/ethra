The green fire along his claws pulsed once, then steadied. <span class="speech">"It is my crown. My throne. My tournament. And I will wager what is mine as I see fit."</span>

He rose from the throne, his white cloak swirling around him, and stepped down from the dais until he stood face-to-face with the High Priest. Solen was old—ancient, even, by Wengari standards—and he had served three generations of Bright Paw kings. But he had never stood this close to the White Dawn before. He had never felt the heat of the green fire radiating from the young king's claws.


<div class="dialogue-block">
<span class="speech">"You are afraid,"</span> Ajani said. It was not an accusation. It was an observation. <span class="speech">"You are afraid that a Humman will win. Or a Veylar. Or a Shadow Paw. You are afraid that no one will win. That no one will dare to challenge me. That the other races will look at the Wengari—at me—and decide that the crown is not worth the risk of facing the White Dawn in single combat."</span>
</div>


He let the silence stretch. Solen's mouth opened, then closed. The other Bright Paw elders stood frozen behind him, their golden manes limp, their claws retracted.


<div class="dialogue-block">
<span class="speech">"You have spent the night whispering about cities given away and vassals made friends,"</span> Ajani continued. <span class="speech">"You have questioned my judgment. You have doubted my wisdom. But you have not once—not once—doubted my strength. And now, when I offer to prove that strength before every race on Ethra, you are terrified. Not because I might lose. </div>


<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">He reached out and placed a single claw gently against Solen's chest, over the old priest's heart. "The sun does not ask permission to shine. </div>


<div class="dialogue-block">

*Warm, unflinching, unforgiving.* His father's words echoed in his mind as he turned away from the stunned elders and walked toward the arena's edge. The hour was almost up. The tournament was about to begin. And the crown of the Wengari was waiting.


In the space of three heartbeats, no one moved. The elders stood with their heads bowed and their claws retracted, and old Solen pressed one paw slowly to his chest, over his heart, where the king's claw had rested. No argument came. No protest followed. The silence was the surrender. The green fire still flickered along Ajani's claws, casting dancing shadows across the ancient stone—dimmed, but not gone.


The arena beyond the throne was silent. The gathered families, who had been watching the confrontation with the same rapt attention they would give to a duel, slowly began to murmur among themselves. The Stripe Paws exchanged glances. The Shadow Paws nodded grimly. The Pyrinae scribes scratched furiously at their parchments. The Humman ambassador clutched her daughter's hand.


And the Veylar Shell-Singer, seated alone in the eastern colonnade, opened both luminous eyes and smiled. The White Dawn had reminded his own blood who ruled the desert. The tournament would begin in minutes. And every Wengari in that arena now understood, with absolute certainty, that their king was not merely strong. He was the sun. Warm. Unflinching. Unforgiving.


*The sun is up in the sky, it's time.*

The arena fell silent. The noon sun of the Steadfast blazed overhead, casting sharp shadows across the sand, and every eye—Wengari, Pyrinae, Humman, Veylar, Threx—turned toward the central arena floor.


One by one, the families presented their champions.


<div class="dialogue-block">

The Shadow Paws came next. Kareth led them—not as a champion, but as an elder escorting his family's chosen. Behind him walked three assassins in the dark ceremonial garb of their order. Their pelts were the color of midnight, their claws lacquered black, their faces unreadable. The young assassin Thane, Varek's chosen, walked at the center—lean, silent, his one remaining ear twitching at every sound. The other two flanked him like shadows given form.

They did not speak. They did not salute. They simply stood, and waited, and let their silence speak for them.


The Bright Paws, still reeling from their confrontation with Ajani, presented their champions with visible hesitation. The High Priest Solen, his ancient paws still trembling, escorted three golden-maned warriors onto the sand. They were the best the Bright Paws could offer—trained in the old forms, disciplined, loyal. But as they saluted the throne, their eyes flickered nervously toward the king who had just threatened to deliver them to the endless night if they failed to meet his standards.


The Motted Paws came last of the Wengari families—three silver-furred warriors whose spotted coats shimmered in the noon light. They were silent as always, their elegant heads inclined toward the throne, their claws sheathed. One of them, a woman named Sylva, met Ajani's eyes for a single, long moment before looking away.


And then came the others.


The Humman delegation, after a frantic, whispered conference, sent forth a single champion—a Stripe Paw mercenary hired at great expense, her striped armor gleaming, her expression one of grim resignation. She had not expected to compete for the crown of the Wengari when she took the Hummans' coin. But here she was. She saluted the throne with a warrior's respect, her claws extended, her eyes steady.


<div class="dialogue-block">

The Quick, floating in its shaded pool, sent a ripple of warmth through the mycelial network that would reach L'vat in the distant marsh—a silent message that the White Dawn had honored the peaceful as well as the strong.


And the Humman ambassador, Seris, clutched her daughter's hand and whispered something that might have been a prayer.


The champions of the five Wengari families stood alone on the sand—twelve warriors, the best their people could offer, waiting for the tournament to begin. The white Styx circled far above, patient as the stars. The green fire flickered gently along Ajani's claws. And the tournament for the crown of the Wengari was about to commence.

The arena watched in silence. The Wengari families, who had never seen the Pyrinae work sorcery on this scale, leaned forward in their seats. The Humman scribes scratched furiously at their parchments. The Veylar Shell-Singer opened her luminous eyes wide, her fin-ridges flaring with interest.


And then the Pyrinae began to build.


It started with a low hum—a vibration that rose from the Vein-Dwellers' staffs and spread through the stone of the arena floor. The sand rippled. The ancient flagstones groaned. Then, with a sound like the cracking of a world, the earth began to move. The basin rose from the arena floor not as a construction but as a revelation—the stone flowing upward like water, shaped by the will of the Pyrinae sorcerers into perfect, seamless walls.

Rune-glass filaments woven into the stone pulsed with amber light, reinforcing the walls, smoothing the edges, creating a basin that was not merely functional but beautiful. The work of centuries, compressed into minutes. The Wengari, who had seen the Pyrinae as vassals for three thousand years, watched in stunned silence as their former servants reshaped the very stone of the arena.


Nefere stood at the edge of the completed basin, her clouded eyes surveying the work. The walls were smooth as polished glass. The corners were sharp as a blade. The depth was exactly three paces—enough to contain a duel, enough to make a fall meaningful, enough to remind every combatant that the earth itself now answered to the White Dawn's allies. She turned to face the throne and bowed her head.

The green fire flickered along Ajani's claws as he considered. Somewhere in the Bright Paw section, the young priest who had railed against the king the night before held his breath. Somewhere in the Shadow Paw alcoves, Kareth leaned forward, his one good eye gleaming. And in the center of the arena, the twelve champions of the Wengari families waited for their king's answer.

That was an invitation.


The Bright Paw champions exchanged uneasy glances. They were trained in the old forms, the ceremonial combat of the royal court. Zara, who had seen more tournaments than she could count, felt something cold run down her spine. She had watched Wengari warriors fight until their claws shattered and their eyes swelled shut, too proud to yield, too stubborn to fall. She had seen champions carried from the arena with wounds that would never fully heal. The king's rule did not merely permit that ancient ferocity. It demanded it.
