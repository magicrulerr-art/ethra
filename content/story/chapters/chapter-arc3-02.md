The Shadow Paw quarter lay in the oldest part of the capital, where the buildings were carved directly into the base of the plateau and the streets were narrow and dark. The family of the First Tyrant had always preferred the shadows, even before the Black Fire gave them reason to hide. Tonight, their elders gathered in a chamber deep beneath the rock, lit only by a single oil lamp that cast their scarred faces in flickering relief.


Kareth was there. The old general had not slept since the council in the inner chamber. His obsidian staff leaned against the wall beside him, and his one good eye moved slowly across the faces of his kin. There were seven of them—the senior-most elders of the Shadow Paw council—and they had been arguing for hours.

The Bright Paw temples stood at the highest point of the capital, a complex of white stone and golden mosaics that caught the first light of the Steadfast every morning. Tonight, the priests who tended those temples were not asleep. They were gathered in the central sanctuary, their golden manes braided with ceremonial ribbons, their voices low and urgent.


The High Priest, a venerable Bright Paw named Solen—no relation to the broken heir who had been drained by the Tree—sat at the center of the gathering, his paws folded in his lap, his eyes closed as if in meditation. But he was not meditating. He was listening.

The pool was cool. The Quick had never felt anything like it—not in the marshes, where the water was brackish and thick with mycelium; not on the long journey across the desert, where every step had been a torture of dry heat and shifting sand. This pool was clear, and calm, and shaded by woven awnings that the Bright Paw servants had erected at the king's command. The Quick floated on its back, its small limbs spread wide, its multifaceted eyes watching the stars wheel slowly overhead.


It was thinking about the king.


L'vat had told the network many things about Ajani Brightmane. That he was stubborn. That he asked too many questions. That he did not know when to kneel and when to stand. That he had saved a Lament's life without knowing what a Lament was, simply because he saw something suffering and could not look away. L'vat had not told the network that the king would be kind. The old root did not believe in kindness.

He believed in bargains, exchanges, mutual obligations that bound one creature to another. Kindness, he had said, was just a bargain where the terms were hidden.


But the Quick had felt the king's fire. It had felt the green flame pulse through its exhausted body, steadying its breathing, easing the tremors in its limbs, warming it in a way that the desert sun could not. That was not a bargain. That was not an exchange. That was simply a gift, given freely, with no terms attached. The Quick did not know what to do with a gift.


It lifted one small limb and watched the green fire flicker along its chitin. The king's fire. The Deep's fire. L'vat's fire. It was all the same fire, in the end—the fire that burned in the heart of the marshes, the fire that connected every Rooted and every Lament and every Quick to the vast, dreaming network of the mycelial deep. The king carried it now. He was part of the network, in a way that no Wengari had ever been.

Not even the Lightbringer, who had walked the March and spoken with the Laments, had carried the green fire in his claws.

The Quick closed its multifaceted eyes. The stars wheeled overhead. The green fire flickered gently along its chitin. And somewhere in the mycelial network, a thousand miles away, an old Lament named L'vat felt a pulse of warmth run through the roots, and wondered what his student had done now.


The northern watchtower was the oldest structure in the capital, older even than the Bright Paw temples, older than the inner chamber where the Lightbringer had vanished. It had been built in the days before the pact, when the desert was still wild and the Wengari huddled in scattered settlements, prey to every predator that roamed the sands. Its stones were worn smooth by the wind, and its parapet offered a clear view of the northern road—the road that stretched across the desert and into the distant green of the Rune-Belt.

Tonight, two watchmen stood on that parapet, their golden manes ruffling in the cool night breeze, their eyes fixed on the horizon.
