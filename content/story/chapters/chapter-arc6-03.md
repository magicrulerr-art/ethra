Outside the great hall, the morning sun was climbing higher over the shattered capital. The wall was still broken. The memorial pillar stood in the eastern plaza, waiting for the celebration. The legion was preparing to march on Verdantis. The Shadow Office was being born. And the king, his scorpions flanking him, was already walking toward the northern gate to work the stone alongside his people.






The wall had become a place of healing. In the weeks since the victory, Ajani had made it his practice to work alongside the laborers every afternoon. He had no skill with stone—the Pyrinae engineers had politely suggested he focus on hauling rather than masonry—but his presence had changed the atmosphere on the scaffolding. If the king could sweat alongside them, so could the generals. If the generals could haul stone, so could the honor guards.

And if the honor guards could work beside the Human refugees, so could everyone else.


The young Human was struggling with a boulder. He was perhaps sixteen, his dark arms thin but wiry, his face still carrying the hollow look of someone who had not eaten well in weeks. He had been one of the civilians who grabbed a table leg and marched to the wall on the morning of the battle, and he had been working the stone ever since, refusing all offers of lighter duty.

The sandstorm descended without warning. The sky turned orange and then black, the wind whipping across the scaffolding with a sound like screaming. Ajani dropped the boulder and pushed the young Human behind him, his body instinctively shielding the boy. Blackie and Reddy were already moving, their tails rising, their white-lacquered pincers clicking with alarm.


And then, just as suddenly, the sandstorm dissipated. Where the swirling dust had been, twenty-one figures now stood at the northern gate. Twenty of them were small—barely chest-height on a Wengari—their iridescent chitin catching the afternoon sun, their multifaceted eyes blinking in the unfamiliar light. The twenty-first was not small. It was a Lament, its chitin dark and gleaming, its multifaceted eyes ancient and patient, its body radiating the quiet, inexorable presence of the Mycelial Deep.

It was the most Threx anyone had ever seen in one place. It was more Threx than most Wengari had seen in a lifetime.


The Threx looked at the Hummans on the wall. Their multifaceted eyes took in the scorpion riders, the merchants, the refugees who had been working alongside the Wengari. And then, as one, the Quick raised their limbs and hissed—a sound of pure, instinctive hostility, the sound of creatures who had been burned by the Plague twice before and remembered.


The young Human behind Ajani went rigid with terror. The scorpion riders on the wall scrambled backward, their hands reaching for weapons they had not carried since the battle. The Wengari soldiers tensed, their claws half-extending, unsure whether to defend the Hummans or stand aside.

For a single, breathless moment, the Wengari on the wall and the Hummans on the scaffolding and the Threx at the gate all stood frozen, unsure whether this was a greeting or an attack.

The Quick were not at ease. Their multifaceted eyes darted across the wall, taking in the Wengari soldiers, the Pyrinae engineers, the scorpion riders who had been cleared by Seris's tribunal. When their gaze fell on the Hummans, they hissed—a low, instinctive sound of fear and recognition. Their limbs raised, their claws extended, their bodies trembling with the effort of restraining themselves. One of them, smaller than the others, let out a thin, reedy sound that might have been a whimper.

They looked ready to strike. They looked ready to flee. They looked like creatures caught between an old terror and an unfamiliar command.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<span class="speech">"These are the ones,"</span> one of the Quick whispered, its voice a thin, reedy rasp. <span class="speech">"These are the ones who burned us. The First Tyrant's fire. The Hummans on the wall had gone very still. The young boy Ajani had been helping with the boulder was pale, his dark eyes wide. The scorpion riders had raised their hands, empty, showing they carried no weapons. The Wengari soldiers had tensed, unsure whether to intervene. Blackie and Reddy had positioned themselves between the Quick and the Hummans, their tails raised, their white-lacquered stingers gleaming. They did not attack. But they were ready.


<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">Ajani rose to his feet. The diadem's gem pulsed with a steady, calming light. "L'vat. Tell them what happened here. Tell them the Hummans fought on the wall. <div class="dialogue-block">

I trained under your Lament. I am your brother, as I told the Quick who came before. And I am telling you—these Hummans are not the ones who burned you. They are the ones who fought with us. They saved Wengari lives. They died for this city. If you cannot accept that, then you have come here for nothing."</p>

<div class="dialogue-block">

Then he extended his limb again—not to strike, but to rest it gently on Ajani's shoulder. <span class="speech">"You always were too trusting. Too willing to see the good in things that should be feared. It is why the Deep chose you. It is also why you nearly died."</span> He turned to the Quick. <span class="speech">"Stand down. The Humman woman was perhaps thirty, her dark hair pulled back in a simple cloth, her arms carrying a basket of dried fruits and flatbread. She had been bringing food to her husband every day since the wall repairs began, weaving through the scaffolding with the practiced ease of someone who had learned to navigate a city under siege. Her daughter trotted beside her, a small girl of perhaps four years, her dark curls bouncing with each step.

The girl had been told to stay close. She had been told to hold her mother's hand. She had been told many things, and she had forgotten all of them the moment she saw the Quick.


The Threx were still standing near the gate, their iridescent chitin catching the afternoon sun in a thousand shifting colors. The girl stopped walking. Her small mouth fell open. Her dark eyes went wide. 'They're so pretty,' she thought.


<div class="dialogue-block">
Her mother reached the scaffolding and began handing the basket up to her husband, who was working alongside a Stripe Paw mason. <span class="speech">"I brought extra for the crew,"</span> she was saying. <span class="speech">"There's enough fruit for—"</span> She turned. Her daughter was no longer beside her. The girl was running. Her small legs pumping, her dark curls streaming behind her, her face alight with the uncomplicated wonder of a child who had seen something beautiful and wanted to be near it. The mother screamed. The father dropped his tools and lunged from the scaffolding. Ajani turned, his hand reaching out, but the girl was too fast, too small, too close to the Threx. The Quick saw her coming. Their multifaceted eyes tracked her approach with something that was not quite fear and not quite aggression—the instinctive tension of creatures who had been burned by the world twice before and had learned to expect fire. The smallest Quick, the one at the edge of the group, raised its limbs. Its claws extended. Its body shuddered with the effort of not striking.
</div>


<div class="dialogue-block">
The girl reached the Quick and threw her arms around its torso. <span class="speech">"You're so pretty!"</span> she said, in the common tongue, her voice bright and clear. She pressed her face against the iridescent chitin, rubbing her cheek against its surface. <span class="speech">"So pretty. Like a gem. Like a rainbow."</span>
</div>


The Quick went absolutely rigid. Every Threx at the gate went rigid. The Lament's multifaceted eyes fixed on the small girl with an expression that was utterly unreadable. The Wengari on the wall had frozen, their claws half-extended, their bodies tensed to intervene. The mother was running, her scream still echoing across the scaffolding. The father was running. Ajani was running. No one would reach her in time.


<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">The girl pulled back from the Quick and reached into the small pouch at her waist. She produced a handful of dried fruit—the same fruit her mother had been carrying in the basket—and held it up toward the Quick's face. She spoke in broken common tongue, her words halting but earnest. "Want? Fruit? It's good. I like the red ones best. But you can have the red ones. You're pretty."</p>
</div>


The Quick stared at the fruit. It stared at the girl. Its claws, still raised, still trembling, slowly lowered. The other Quick watched. The Lament watched. The entire wall watched. And the smallest Quick, the one who had been afraid since it crossed the desert's edge, the one who had hissed at the Hummans only moments before, extended one trembling limb and gently took the fruit from the girl's hand. It did not eat it.

It simply held it, its multifaceted eyes blinking, its body still shuddering with the effort of processing what was happening.


<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">The mother reached her daughter and scooped her up, backing away, her eyes wide with terror. The girl waved over her mother's shoulder. "Bye, pretty! Come back! I'll have more fruit!" The Quick did not wave back. But it did not hiss. And it was still holding the fruit.</p>
</div>





<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">Ajani was already moving, positioning himself between the Threx and the Hummans, his paws raised in a gesture of peace. His voice came out higher than he intended. "I apologize for our guests! Human cubs are very curious! She meant no offense! She's four! She doesn't know—"</p>
</div>


L'vat raised one limb, cutting him off. The ancient Lament's multifaceted eyes were fixed on the smallest Quick, who was still holding the dried fruit, still blinking, still trembling slightly. The other Quick had not lowered their limbs entirely, but they were no longer poised to strike. They were staring at their companion with expressions that, on their alien features, could only be described as utter bewilderment.


<div class="dialogue-block">
<span class="speech">"She gave you food,"</span> L'vat said to the Quick. His voice was quiet now, the stern fury faded into something more measured. <span class="speech">"She called you pretty. She was not afraid. She did not know she should be afraid."</span> He paused. <span class="speech">"What do you feel?"</span>
</div>


<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">The Quick looked down at the fruit in its limb. Its voice, when it came, was a thin, reedy whisper. "Confused. She is small. She is warm. She did not burn us. I do not understand."</p>
</div>


<div class="dialogue-block">
<span class="speech">"Neither do I,"</span> L'vat said. <span class="speech">"But you did not strike her. That is progress."</span>
</div>


<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">Ajani lowered his paws slightly, still watching the Quick with the wariness of someone who had nearly witnessed a diplomatic catastrophe. "She's the daughter of one of the masons. Her father fought on the wall. He was one of the civilians who grabbed a table leg and marched to the gate. They're not—they're not what you remember. I know that's hard to believe. But she just proved it."</p>
</div>


<div class="dialogue-block">
L'vat turned his ancient gaze toward the small girl, who was still waving over her mother's shoulder. <span class="speech">"Yes. She did."</span> He looked back at Ajani. <span class="speech">"The Deep will want a full report. It will be very confused by this. It has spent five thousand years teaching us that Hummans are dangerous. Now I must tell it that a Humman cub gave one of our Quick a piece of fruit and called it pretty. The Deep does not handle confusion well. You owe me, disciple."</span>
</div>





L'vat had not stopped talking since they passed through the northern gate. The ancient Lament walked beside Ajani, his dark chitin gleaming in the afternoon sun, his multifaceted eyes taking in every detail of the city—the broken wall, the Pyrinae engineers, the Human refugees, the scaffolding that still clung to the eastern section like a bandage on an open wound. And he had something to say about all of it.


<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"Your form was wrong. In the memories you showed me—the summoning, the chants, the elemental convergence—your form was wrong. The fire spirit's binding sequence should have been anchored to your dominant arm, not your off-hand. The ice spirit's containment ring was too tight; if the battle had lasted another two minutes, you would have lost your arm. The light spirit's invocation was missing the third stanza of the Seraphic Hymn—the one that stabilizes the manifestation. You didn't use the full formulas, did you? You improvised. You improvised six elemental summonings in the middle of a battle, and you didn't even complete the training I gave you."</p>
</div>


<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">Ajani smiled and nodded. "I was unconscious for three weeks before that. I didn't have time to review my notes."</p>
</div>


<div class="dialogue-block">
<span class="speech">"Your notes."</span> L'vat's voice was flat with disdain. <span class="speech">"You should have finished your training. I told you when you left the March that you were not ready. I told you the spirit formulas were incomplete. I told you the old names were dangerous. And you—"</span> He gestured at the shattered wall. <span class="speech">"You summoned all six anyway. While burning your own aura. Without completing the binding sequences. Without the stabilization hymns. Without a single anchor stone. And it worked. I do not understand how it worked. The Deep does not understand how it worked.
