## Chapter 8: 08:45 — Scorpions Still Marching

It was 8:45 in the morning. The second contingent of suicide scorpions was still marching toward the gate. The Plague mist was still rising from the glassed sand. And the young generals had just learned that the war was not merely an invasion. It was a betrayal.

The Humman command tent had grown quiet. The war drums still pounded outside, but they were distant now, a ritual rather than a signal. The golden light of the shattered dome had faded, and the morning sun streamed through the tent flaps, catching the dust motes that hung in the still air. Mekhmed the Third stood at the campaign table, his silk robes disheveled, his jeweled fingers drumming against the wood. He was not smiling anymore.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<span class="speech">"Your Wealthiness."</span> The general who spoke was young—promoted in the chaos after Tamsin's arrest and the old general's execution—and his voice was carefully neutral. <span class="speech">"How many more suicide scorpions do you have?"</span>
</div>

<div class="dialogue-block">
Mekhmed grimaced. He looked down at his fingers as if counting, though the number was already seared into his mind. <span class="speech">"Only fifty left."</span> The generals breathed. It was not relief—not quite—but it was something close. Fifty was enough. Fifty was a reserve. Fifty was a threat that could be held back, saved for the right moment, rather than thrown at the wall in desperation. The young general stepped forward. <span class="speech">"Then order them back, Your Wealthiness. They are our trump card. We need to save them. If the Wengari send more reinforcements—and they will, they always do—we will need every weapon we have. The suicide scorpions are the only thing they fear."</span>
</div>

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">Mekhmed looked at him. For a long moment, the tent was silent. Then, in a rare display of something that might have been understanding, he nodded. "Order them back. Pull the vanguard to the perimeter. We hold position. For now." The generals bowed and departed, their relief barely concealed. Outside the tent, the order was given. The second contingent of suicide scorpions—fifty riders, their mounts still fresh, their dark eyes still closed in grim resignation—turned from the wall and retreated. The Wengari defenders watched them go, their claws still raised, their hearts still pounding. No one cheered. The retreat was not a surrender. It was a pause.</p>
</div>

<div class="dialogue-block">
An old general—promoted twice in the past hour, his scarred face weary beyond measure—stood at the edge of the Humman perimeter and looked out across the glassed sand. The orange mist still hung low over the craters where the suicide scorpions had detonated. The bodies of the ghosts still lay where they had fallen. The Plague had blanketed the sand, and the Wengari could not cross it. <span class="speech">"They can't come for us,"</span> the general said quietly to no one in particular. <span class="speech">"The Plague has sealed the approach. We can go for them—we still have the scorpions, the Amuks, the Tide Wolves—but they cannot come for us. Not until the mist clears. Not until the Plague fades. We have an hour. Perhaps more. Let's just hope he doesn't notice."</span>
</div>
