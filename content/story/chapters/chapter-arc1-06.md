## Chapter 6: The Passing

*The thorned root withdrew, leaving a single drop of amber sap on the tip of the green spear—a mark, a binding, a deadline.*


*"Go now. Your father is dying. Your people are waiting. The Lament will carry word of what has happened here to every corner of Ethra. And the one in the Rune-Belt—the one Velarius woke—he will hear of it too. He will know that a White Dawn has risen. He will know that the pact has been renewed. And he will come for you, as he came for the Lightbringer, as he came for all the others. Do not let him find you unprepared."*


*The blossom began to close, its petals folding inward like a fist. The green luminescence faded. The roots withdrew into the rock. And the Tree's voice echoed one last time through the chamber—cold, ancient, utterly without mercy.*


*"You are a king, Ajani Brightmane. Kings do not bow. But kings also do not fail. Not twice. Not when the price is everything."*

I'll take the seed and run with it. The Tree's final words are perfectly calibrated—ancient, patient, and utterly without sentiment. No hope offered. No encouragement given. Just a pact brokered, a question posed, and a gift bestowed.


Let me narrate what happens next.


The rumble began deep beneath the chamber, a seismic groan that vibrated through the roots and the stone and the very air. Dust sifted from the ceiling. The amber pool rippled, then stilled. The blossom closed completely, its petals folding inward like a fist releasing its grip on the world.


And then, from above, came the sounds.


A roar—not of any beast Ajani had ever heard, but something deeper, older, more resonant. It was answered by another, and another, until the air itself seemed to shake with the chorus. Beneath the roars, a higher sound: the panicked shouts of Wengari, the clatter of rune-glass staves as the Pyrinae scrambled, the distant thunder of Stripe Paw tails beating against stone in alarm or awe. And beneath even that, a single, sustained note—the Lament's hum, no longer soft and patient, but triumphant, ecstatic, a song of recognition.



The passage before Ajani blazed with golden light, and he climbed. The stairs seemed shorter now, the air warmer, the weight of the Tree's presence receding with every step. When he emerged into the Chamber of Reflection, his father was waiting. Uthgard IX stood—stood, despite the centuries and the withered hand and the dying light within him—with his eyes fixed on the passage and his good hand pressed against his heart.


<div class="dialogue-block">
<span class="speech">"Ajani."</span> His voice was hoarse, barely a whisper. <span class="speech">"What did you do?"</span>
</div>


But before Ajani could answer, the sound came again—that roar, closer now, shaking the very walls of the palace. Uthgard's eyes widened. He knew that sound. He had heard it only once before, centuries ago, when he was young and the world was still full of mysteries.


<div class="dialogue-block">
<span class="speech">"The Styx,"</span> he breathed. <span class="speech">"The Styx are here."</span>
</div>


He looked at his son—not as a king regarding his heir, but as a father seeing something impossible.


<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"They have not come to the capital since the Lightbringer vanished. Ajani... what did the Tree give you?"</p>
</div>


And then the doors of the palace above burst open, and a Wengari herald—a young Bright Paw with a voice cracking with terror and wonder—shouted into the depths:


<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"My king! My prince! Come quickly! The sky—the sky is full of fire!"</p>
</div>


The mist swirled at the arena's edge, thick and golden and utterly wrong—not the dry heat-haze of the desert, but something damp, living, breathing. It coiled around the feet of the gathered Wengari like a living thing, and where it touched, the sand hissed and steamed.


Above, the sky was full of Styx.


Dozens of them—perhaps hundreds—circling the arena in a great, silent vortex. Their plumage blazed with the full spectrum of the Flicker's fire: crimson and amber, deep violet and pale gold, and here and there, a flash of the rarest hue, the Maltese blue that belonged only to the males. They did not dive. They did not hunt. They simply flew, and watched, and waited.


And at the center of the arena, where the Four Pillars had stood for a thousand years, the mist began to take shape.


It was vast—easily the size of a Lament, but where the Lament was chitin and iridescence and the memory of a person, this was something else entirely. Fur. Claws. Eyes that burned with a cold, green fire. It stepped forward out of the mist, and the mist came with it, clinging to its flanks like a cloak, and the assembled races of Ethra—Wengari, Pyrinae, Hummans, even the silent, watching Lament—fell utterly, completely silent.


The creature was a Tide Wolf.


But no Tide Wolf had ever been this size. No Tide Wolf had ever burned with the cold fire of the Kyre Tree's ancient light. And no Tide Wolf had ever walked out of the desert mist with the Styx circling above it like attendants before a king.


It stopped at the edge of the arena's central sand, its massive head swiveling slowly, surveying the gathered races with an intelligence that was not animal, not sentient, but something in between—something that had been waiting a very long time for this moment.


Then it lifted its head and howled. The sound was not a wolf's howl. It was the sound of the desert itself—the grinding of stone, the whisper of sand, the patient, hungry silence that had endured since before the first Wengari walked the world. And when the howl faded, the creature turned its burning green eyes toward the palace gate, where Ajani Brightmane stood with his father at his side.


It lowered its head. It bent one massive foreleg. And the Tide Wolf, the apex predator of the Rune-Belt, the creature that had never bowed to anyone or anything in the history of Ethra—bowed to the young king.


The Lament raised its arms to the sky and began to sing—a song of the Deep, of the Tree, of the road that would stretch from the desert to the sea, of the pact renewed and the White Dawn risen. The Styx answered, their fires flaring in unison, painting the sky in shades of gold and green and blue. And the gathered races—the proud Wengari, the patient Pyrinae, the terrified Hummans, the silent, watching Lament—all turned their eyes toward the young king who had done what no king had done since the Lightbringer vanished.



Uthgard IX, his withered hand trembling, his golden eyes wet with tears he had not shed in centuries, placed his good hand on his son's shoulder.


<div class="dialogue-block">
<span class="speech">"Ajani,"</span> he whispered, <span class="speech">"what did the Tree give you?"</span>
</div>


And Ajani, watching the Tide Wolf bow before him, watching the Styx paint the sky, watching the Lament sing a song of beginnings, answered his father with the truth he had only just begun to understand.


<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"Not a gift. A herald."</p>
</div>


*'what in the world?! ive never seen so many styx before, and this.. mist what is going on?, is that? what is that thing?'*


He stops before Ajani, and he smiles. It is not the weary smile of a dying king. It is the proud smile of a father who has watched his son become something greater than he ever dared to hope.


<div class="dialogue-block">
<span class="speech">"My son. My White Dawn. My king."</span> His voice is soft, meant only for Ajani, though in the silence of the arena it carries to every ear. <span class="speech">"I have kept the pact for four hundred and seventy-three years. I have bled for it. I have withered for it. And now, at last, I am free."</span>
</div>


He raises his right hand—the hand that still works—and places it on Ajani's chest, over his heart.


<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"The Lightbringer's fire passes from king to heir. It has done so since Uthgard the Second took up the mantle when the first vanished into the Chamber of Reflection. It is not Aura. It is not Sorcery. It is something older—a fragment of the White Dawn that the Lightbringer left behind, passed down through the bloodline, renewed with each generation, diminished with each passing year. I am the last to carry it. You are the first to renew it."</p>
</div>


His hand begins to glow—a soft, golden light, the last ember of the Lightbringer's gift.


<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"I give it to you now. Not as a burden. Not as a curse. As a gift. The last gift of a dying king to his only son."</p>
</div>


The light flows from his hand into Ajani's chest, and Uthgard IX, four hundred and seventy-three years old, survivor of two Crimson Tides, keeper of the pact, father of the White Dawn, closes his eyes.


He does not burn like the Styx. He does not burst into flame or dissolve into light. He simply... fades. His body becomes translucent, then transparent, then nothing at all. The last thing to vanish is his smile.


And when it is done, when the old king is gone and the new king stands alone at the center of the arena, the Four Pillars blazing around him, the Styx circling above, the Lament humming below, the gathered races of Ethra know—with a certainty that goes deeper than bone—that the world has changed. The White Dawn has risen. The pact is renewed. And the age of Ajani Brightmane has begun.
