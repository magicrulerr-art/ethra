## Chapter 7: 08:40 — The Plague Comes

It was 8:40 in the morning, the seventh day of the Month of Storms, in the first year of the reign of Ajani Brightmane. The Plague was coming. And the gate was about to fall.

Drums were heard from the sky. Not the low, pounding war drums of the Humman army, but something older, something rhythmic and resonant that vibrated through the bones rather than the air. The defenders on the wall looked up, and at first they saw nothing—only the pale morning sky, empty and indifferent. Then the arrows came.

They appeared out of nowhere, loosed from empty air, each shaft finding its mark with impossible precision. A suicide scorpion rider took an arrow through the chest and detonated in a pillar of fire ten meters tall, the blast consuming him and his mount before they could reach the wall. Another rider fell. Another. The second contingent of suicide scorpions was being dismantled from above, each detonation safely distant from the gate, each pillar of fire erupting in the empty sand rather than against the ancient stone.

A glass line formed in the desert—a jagged, glittering scar of fused sand that marked the edge of the kill zone—and above it, a faint orange mist began to rise, the residual aura of the Plague weapons venting into the morning air.

M'rak knew that sound. The drums. The bone-vibration rhythm of the Motted Paw communication system, the ancient language that the ghosts understood. The ghosts had arrived. A hundred bats, five-meter wingspans, optical camouflage, silent and deadly. They were firing from above, their archers invisible, their arrows striking the suicide riders before they could reach the wall. For a moment—a brief, glorious moment—the gate was safe.

Then a ghost flickered into visibility and fell. It tumbled from the sky in silence, its great wings limp, its body trailing a faint wisp of orange mist as it crashed into the sand. Another ghost appeared above it, its camouflage flickering like a dying candle, and then it too fell. A third. A fourth. A fifth.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">The drums faltered. A voice—Yvaria's voice, the young Motted Paw general who had ridden through the night declaring she would be the king's bride—screamed across the sky. "STAY CLEAR FROM THE DEATH! THE MIST IS POISON! STAY CLEAR!" But it was too late. The ghosts had flown through the orange haze to loose their arrows, and the Plague was already in them. They fell in silence. They fell in clusters. They fell until the sky was empty of their camouflage and the sand was littered with their broken bodies. Thirty-five ghosts died in the span of three minutes. Every Wengari on the wall, every Humman on the battlefield, every Pyrinae sorcerer and Stripe Paw defender and Shadow Paw assassin stood frozen, staring at the carnage.</p>
</div>

<div class="dialogue-block">
In the Humman command tent, Mekhmed was laughing. <span class="speech">"Stupid brutes! See what happens when you mess with me? They thought they could hide in the sky! They thought their camouflage would protect them! The Plague does not care about camouflage. The Plague does not care about anything."</span> An old general—a veteran of the border wars whose dark skin was cross-hatched with scars even older than Tamsin's—stepped forward. His voice was hoarse with horror. <span class="speech">"Are you mad? You're unleashing the Plague! All the races will hunt us for this! The Wengari, the Pyrinae, the Threx, the Veylar—they will never stop! They will exterminate us! You have doomed our entire people!"</span>
</div>

<div class="dialogue-block">
Mekhmed turned. His smile was gone. His face was utterly, terrifyingly serious. <span class="speech">"Only if they know."</span> He looked at the guards stationed at the entrance of the tent. <span class="speech">"Kill him. And kill anyone else who speaks of what they saw today. The Plague is not here. The Plague never existed. What happened on this battlefield was a Wengari weapon that malfunctioned. That is the story. That is what we will tell. Now kill him."</span>
</div>

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">The guards hesitated. They were young men, their dark eyes wide with the same horror that had frozen every creature on the battlefield. But they were oath-bound. They drew their blades, and the old general did not resist. He simply looked at Mekhmed with an expression of infinite contempt and said, "Your father would be ashamed." Then the blades fell, and the tent was silent, and the drums from the sky had stopped.</p>
</div>

The ghosts landed in silence. What remained of them—sixty-five of the hundred that had flown through the night, their great wings folding against their bodies, their optical camouflage flickering with exhaustion and grief. They settled near the surviving Wohs, near the smoking craters where the suicide scorpions had detonated, near the glassed sand and the faint orange mist that still hung in the air like a warning.

Yvaria dismounted before her ghost had fully touched the ground. Her young face, so bright with fierce determination when she had ridden through the darkness declaring she would be the king's bride, was pale now. Her silver-furred hands were trembling. She walked toward M'rak, who was still standing amid the wreckage of his own command—five Wohs remaining out of twenty, his burn-scarred arms limp at his sides, his golden armor scorched and dented.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"Tell me that isn't what I think it is."</p>
</div>

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">Her voice was quiet. It was the quiet of someone who already knew the answer and was asking anyway, because the answer was too terrible to accept without confirmation. But it was not M'rak who answered. Tamsin, the Humman general, still standing beside her lathered Tide Wolf with her scarred hands raised, spoke before he could. "It's Velarius Plague. We didn't know. We were never..."</p>
</div>

She did not finish. M'rak's hand closed around her throat. He lifted her off the ground with a single, effortless motion, his claws pressing into the soft flesh of her neck, his golden eyes burning with a fury that was not cold at all. When he spoke, his voice was low and tortured, the voice of a soldier who had watched his comrades consumed by fire and then learned the fire was only the beginning.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"You brought that filth into our home. You brought the Plague. The Fifth Tyrant's filth. Into our city. Near our families. Near our king." <span class="speech-attr">His grip tightened. Tamsin's scarred face was turning pale, her dark eyes wide but unstruggling. She did not fight. She had come here to warn them. She had come here to defect. And the Wengari general whose soldiers had been burned alive by her king's madness was about to kill her for it.</span></p>
</div>

In the inner chamber beneath the palace, the roots of the Kyre Tree were writhing. The lord of the desert, the Golden Sun, the ancient predator that had made a pact with Ajani Brightmane, was in pain. The Plague had touched its domain. The faint orange mist that had risen from the suicide scorpions' detonations had seeped into the sand, into the stone, into the ancient roots that stretched through the bedrock below the capital.

The Tree had felt the First Tyrant's fire five thousand years ago. It had felt the Fifth Tyrant's Plague five hundred years ago. It knew this poison. It remembered.

The roots thrashed against the stone walls of the chamber, their pale green light flickering erratically, their slow, patient hunger replaced by something that looked almost like agony. The blossom at the center of the chamber, the great flower that had opened to speak with Ajani when he first renewed the pact, was trembling. The amber pool beneath it was boiling. And the Tree, which had never screamed, which had never made a sound in all the millions of years it had endured beneath the desert, was screaming now—a silent, psychic shriek that reverberated through the stone, through the palace, through the bodies of every Wengari who carried its mark.

In the war room, Sylva felt it and doubled over. In the temple, Solen felt it and clutched his chest. In the royal chamber, Black Fire and Red Fire felt it and pressed themselves closer to Ajani's unconscious form, their small bodies vibrating with a distress they could not name.

The lord of the desert was screaming. And the great war was not yet over.

The war room was thick with the smell of old stone and older fear. The maps on the granite table were still scattered from the earlier councils, marked with the positions of forces that had already been decimated. The golden light of the destroyed dome had faded, and the morning sun streamed through the high windows, indifferent to the chaos below. M'rak stood at the head of the table. His golden armor was scorched and dented, his burn-scarred arms still trembling with the aftershock of the fire pillars.

He had lost fifteen Wohs in the span of minutes. He had watched his soldiers consumed by a weapon he had only ever heard about in old stories, a weapon that should not exist, a weapon that had been buried with the Fifth Tyrant five hundred years ago. He was not in a mood to be diplomatic.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"Why didn't you call for the army? Why weren't we informed about the king's condition? Why are the walls not manned? I've just lost twenty good Wohs and even better soldiers. You better have an explanation."</p>
</div>

His voice was low and tortured, the same voice he had used when he lifted Tamsin by the throat and nearly killed her for bringing the Plague into his home. He was looking at Sylva. The regent sat at the edge of the granite table, her silver aura still flickering erratically, her ancient eyes hollow from three weeks without proper sleep. She had not spoken since Vasha had thrown the documents onto the table and revealed that her own maid had betrayed them all.

<div class="dialogue-block">
Yvaria stepped forward. Her young face was no longer bright with fierce determination. Her silver-furred hands were still trembling from watching her ghosts fall from the sky. <span class="speech">"Sylva, you're the regent. I saw you win the tournament. How could you let this happen?"</span> She gestured toward the window, toward the burning wall, toward the glassed sand and the orange mist and the bodies of her ghosts. <span class="speech">"No army has marched on the desert. It's unforgiving. The routes are guarded. The wells are watched. How did they know which way to take? How did they know the guard rotations? How did they know the king was unconscious?"</span>
</div>

Vasha answered. She did not speak loudly. She did not need to. She simply raised one claw and pointed at the documents still scattered across the granite table. The copies of the letters. The maps of the capital, annotated in meticulous detail. The guard schedules. The times of day when the watch changed and the wall was most vulnerable. All of it written in the precise, careful hand of a Humman scribe.

M'rak picked up one of the papers. His golden eyes scanned the handwriting, the details, the intimate knowledge of the capital's defenses that could only have come from someone inside the palace. He looked at Sylva. Yvaria looked at the papers, then at Vasha, then at Sylva. The two young generals—one Bright Paw, one Motted Paw, both of whom had ridden through the night to save a city that had been betrayed from within—stood in the war room and waited for an explanation that would never come.

The regent of the Wengari, who had been patient for so long, who had held the capital together through the tournament and the offices and the Golden Sun, who had been teaching Lena to scrub stone channels and sit in the garden and learn patience, sat at the edge of the table with her silver aura guttering like a dying candle. She did not speak. She could not.
