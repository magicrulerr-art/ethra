## Chapter 10: 09:30 — The Wall Breaks

It was 9:30 in the morning. The wall was broken. The purple mist was spreading. And the great war had just become something far worse.

Yvaria's voice cut through the chaos like a blade. She was aloft on her ghost, her drum harness strapped across her chest, her silver-furred paws clutching a rune-glass communication shell that amplified her commands across the wall and the city beyond.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"RETREAT! RETREAT FROM THE WALL! CORDON THE ZONE—SIX SPEARS RADIUS! NO ONE GETS NEAR!"</p>
</div>

The purple orb was still growing. Its tendrils of violet light had already consumed forty Wengari, their bodies suspended in the mist like insects in amber, their screams neither alive nor dead. The mist was spreading along the rampart, curling down the inner face of the wall toward the courtyard below, and the defenders who had survived the initial blast were scrambling backward, their claws scraping against the ancient stone.

The orb pulsed, and the purple light deepened, and another tendril stretched toward the gate.

M'rak was no longer on the wall. He had run through the chaos, his burn-scarred arms pumping, his golden armor clashing against his chest, until he reached the eastern courtyard where Nefere and her Hydromancers were raising something from the sand. It was a tower—slender and elegant, its surface gleaming with the deep, iridescent luster of rune-glass that had been allowed to mature for a thousand years.

The crystals embedded in its frame were ancient, their facets catching the morning sun and throwing it back in a thousand fractured sparks of amber and gold. The Hydromancers were chanting, their rune-glass staves planted in a circle around the tower's base, their voices weaving a complex, interlocking pattern of sorcery that made the air hum with tension.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">Nefere stood at the center of the circle, her clouded eyes fixed on the tower, her rune-glass staff humming with the same deep, resonant frequency as the ancient crystals. M'rak reached her side, gasping for breath. "Will it work?"</p>
</div>

<div class="dialogue-block">
She did not look at him. <span class="speech">"I don't know. We barely had any time to test it. It's a rush job."</span> Her voice was calm, but her ancient paws were trembling on her staff. <span class="speech">"When my network intercepted the letters to the Hummans, we started working on it. It was meant for defense—a concentrated beam of solar energy, focused through the sand crystals. The crystals we used had been maturing for a thousand years, buried in the deep veins beneath the Pyrinae district. We only had enough for one."</span>
</div>

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">A younger Hydromancer, her copper skin slick with sweat, raised her staff. "It's ready."</p>
</div>

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">Nefere nodded. She did not hesitate. She had been waiting for this moment since the first scorpion rider appeared on the horizon, and she had known, with the cold certainty of someone who had spent a lifetime preparing for disasters, that the tower was their last hope. "Fire."</p>
</div>

The tower began to glow. It started as a faint shimmer at its apex, a point of golden light that pulsed once, twice, and then steadied into a brilliant, blinding radiance. The light intensified, and the air around the tower began to shimmer with heat, and then the beam shot forth—a concentrated lance of pure solar energy, focused through the thousand-year-old crystals, aimed directly at the purple orb that was consuming the wall.

The orb resisted. For a few seconds that stretched like hours, the violet light and the golden light warred against each other, their energies colliding in a soundless, terrible struggle that made the air itself scream. Then the beam broke through. The orb collapsed inward, its tendrils retracting, its violet light guttering like a candle in a storm. The Wengari who had been trapped in its grip—those who were still alive, those who were still screaming—fell to the shattered rampart and did not move.

The purple mist evaporated, burned away by the solar lance, and the blackened sand at the base of the wall was vaporized as well, leaving only a deep, jagged scar in the desert floor.

The eastern section of the wall was gone. The ancient stone had not merely cracked or crumbled; it had been erased, carved away by the beam's passage as if by a sculptor's chisel. The gap was twenty meters wide, a wound in the city's defenses that would take years to repair. But the orb was gone. The Plague mist was gone. The immediate threat was over.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">The Hydromancers were screaming. "TONE IT DOWN! TONE IT DOWN! IT WILL BREAK!"</p>
</div>

The tower was shuddering. The ancient crystals in its frame were flickering, their facets cracking under the strain, their thousand-year-old lattices beginning to fracture. Nefere raised her staff and shouted a command in the old tongue, and the beam flickered and died. The tower fell silent. The crystals were dark, their inner light extinguished, their surfaces spider-webbed with cracks. The Hydromancers slumped over their staves, gasping for breath, their bodies trembling with exhaustion.

The mirror array had worked. It would never work again.

The tower fell silent. The beam had faded, the purple orb was gone, and the blackened sand was vaporized. The Hydromancers slumped over their staves, their bodies trembling with exhaustion, their rune-glass crystals dark and cracked. Nefere stood at the center of the circle, her clouded eyes fixed on the smoldering gap where the eastern section of the wall had been. She did not ask if the mirror array had worked. She knew it had worked. She asked the only question that mattered now.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"How many more?"</p>
</div>

The Hydromancers were too exhausted to speak. They simply raised their paws, each one extending a single finger.

One. They had one shot left. The thousand-year-old crystals were nearly spent. The tower would fire once more, and then it would shatter, and the ancient sorcery that had been passed from High Speaker to High Speaker since the time of the Third Tyrant would be gone forever.

Nefere looked at the single finger raised by each of her exhausted sorcerers. She did not speak. She did not need to. One shot. One chance to strike the Humman army before the remaining Plague weapons could be deployed. One chance to turn the tide of the battle. After that, the mirror array would be nothing but broken crystal and fading light. She turned back to the tower and began to prepare.

The Humman command tent was silent. No screams from the battlefield, no war drums, no pounding of Amuk artillery. Just the quiet, ragged breathing of a king who had been promised victory and was now watching his army crumble. Mekhmed the Third sat on his campaign chair, his silk robes disheveled, his jeweled fingers steepled before his face. When he spoke, his voice was almost a whisper.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"Bring me the woman."</p>
</div>

The guards dragged Lena into the tent. She was small—smaller than any Wengari, small even for a Humman—her dark hair disheveled, her scribe's robes torn at the knee where she had stumbled on the desert sand. She had been kept in a supply tent since the battle began, waiting for news of the victory she had made possible. She had heard the war drums. She had heard the cannons. She had heard the screams. She had not known what any of it meant until the guards came for her.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">Mekhmed did not rise. He simply looked at her, his dark eyes cold and still. "Did you not write that they would be defenseless? That they only had a hundred soldiers at most? What was the tower? Why didn't you tell me any of this?"</p>
</div>

<div class="dialogue-block">
Lena was trembling. Her dark eyes darted around the tent, searching the faces of the generals for some flicker of mercy, some hint of understanding. She found none. Every face was closed. Every pair of eyes was hard. They had lost four hundred scorpions. They had lost eight Amuks. They had watched the ghosts fall from the sky and the Woh riders sweep through their lines and the purple orb consume its victims without discrimination. They had followed their king into a disaster, and they needed someone to blame. <span class="speech">"Your—your Wealthiness."</span> Lena's voice was barely a whisper. <span class="speech">"I wrote the truth! I couldn't—how could I know about the tower? I was only a scribe. I served the regent. I copied her correspondence. I saw the guard rotations. I saw the maps. I told you everything I saw. I couldn't have known—"</span>
</div>

<div class="dialogue-block">
<span class="speech">"SILENCE."</span> Mekhmed's voice cracked across the tent like a whip. <span class="speech">"Guards. Silence her. Forever."</span>
</div>

For a moment, no one moved. The generals stood frozen, their faces pale. The guards hesitated, their hands on their blades. Lena stared at the king, her dark eyes wide with the sudden, terrible understanding of what he had just ordered. She opened her mouth to speak—to beg, to explain, to plead—but no words came.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">One of the guards stepped forward, his sword raised high. Lena fell to her knees. "Please! Please, I only wanted to help my people! I only wanted to save us from the Wengari! They're brutes, they're monsters, I saw them kill a man with a flick of their wrist, I was afraid, I was always afraid, please—"</p>
</div>

The guard brought the sword down. The tent was silent again. Mekhmed did not look at the body. He turned back to his campaign table, his jeweled fingers tracing the maps of a city he had failed to conquer. The generals stood in their loose semicircle, their faces carefully blank, their eyes avoiding the small, crumpled form on the tent floor. The letters had been written. The war had been launched. The Plague had been unleashed.

And the woman who had started it all, the frightened scribe who had only wanted to save her people from monsters, had just been executed by the king she had tried to serve. The war drums did not pound. The cannons did not fire. The truce held, fragile and temporary, and the great war was not yet over.

Vex smelled it first. His nose stiffened, his dark ears swiveling forward, his pupils dilating as the scent hit him. It was not a Wengari scent. It was not Humman or Pyrinae or any of the other races that walked the desert. It was older. Deeper. The scent of deep water and ancient patience and something that had been walking for a very long time. He drew his claws. The lacquered tips gleamed with a faint purple glow—the mark of the Shadow Paw poisons, the trade of his order, the tools he had carried since he was old enough to hold a blade.

Lira saw him move and drew her own claws. The green glow of her poison matched his, and her dark eyes swept the corridor with the cold, practiced assessment of a master of the Eight Points. She had been guarding the king's chambers since the second assassination attempt, and she had not left her post. She had listened to the battle rage outside, the cannons and the fireballs and the screams, and she had not moved. Her duty was to the door. Her duty was to the king.

They looked at each other. No words passed between them. None were needed. The look said everything: someone is here.

Vex sniffed again. The scent was stronger now, and something in his hindbrain, something ancient and primal, recognized it. Not from experience. From instinct. From the old stories the Shadow Paw elders told in the darkness of Xhilva, when the cubs were learning to fear the things that lurked beyond the canopy. His pupils dilated further. His claws trembled. He knew what was coming. He knew he could not stop it.

He raked his claws across his own arm. It was a desperate act—the act of an assassin who understood that his own poison might be the only weapon fast enough to strike the thing that was approaching. But it was already too late. His vision blurred. His legs gave way. He crumpled to the stone floor without a sound, his dark pelt pooling around him like a shadow.

Lira saw him fall. Her own claws were still raised, her own body still poised to strike, but her pupils were dilating too, and the green glow on her lacquered claws was flickering. She did not scratch herself. She did not have time. She simply dropped, her body folding onto the stone beside Vex's, her dark fur blending with the shadows of the corridor.

The corridor was silent. The door to the royal chamber was unguarded. And something ancient and patient stepped out of the darkness and approached the king.

The ground trembled again, but this time the rhythm was different. It was not the ponderous, earth-shaking stride of the Amuks or the skittering charge of the scorpions. It was the thunder of Wohs—thirty of them, the western garrison's finest, their natural plate armor gleaming in the morning sun, their gravity-manipulating auras pulsing around them like heat haze. General Reva rode at their head, her striped mane streaming behind her, her golden eyes fixed on the capital.

She had ridden through the night from the river basin, pushing her Wohs to their limits, and now she could see the walls. She could see the gate. She could see the hole.

The eastern section of the wall was gone. A gaping wound, twenty meters wide, its edges still smoking, its stone blackened and cracked. The bodies of Wengari soldiers lay where they had fallen, their golden and striped and shadowed pelts matted with blood and dust. The purple mist was gone, burned away by the mirror array, but the scar remained. Reva's heart stopped. She had been expecting to reinforce a defending army. She had not been expecting to arrive at a city that was already broken.

Then she heard the drums. The bone-vibration rhythm of the ghosts, the Motted Paw communication system, the ancient language that the bats understood. And beneath the drums, a faint, desperate voice—Yvaria's voice, amplified by a rune-glass shell, screaming across the battlefield.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"TURN BACK! TURN BACK! THE ORB IS COMING! TURN—"</p>
</div>

It was too late. A purple orb was already approaching. It came from the Humman lines, a sphere of sickly violet light no larger than a clenched fist, trailing tendrils of purple mist. It moved with a speed that should not have been possible, silent and terrible, and Reva watched it pass her. She watched it fly past her Woh, past her riders, past the vanguard of her command, and then it stopped. It hovered for a single, breathless moment above the center of her formation. Then it detonated.

The blast was not an explosion. It was an unfolding—a silent, terrible blossoming of violet light that expanded outward in concentric rings, consuming everything it touched. Five Wohs were struck in the first heartbeat, their natural plate armor no defense against the Plague-touched sorcery, their massive bodies dissolving into the purple light. Then ten. Then fifteen. The riders on their backs died with them, their screams swallowed by the silence of the orb.

It took only three seconds. Perhaps four. The wails of the dying Wohs—deep, resonant, the sound of creatures that had never known fear and were now experiencing it for the first and last time—echoed across the desert. The riders who survived, fifteen out of thirty, scattered in every direction, their Wohs bucking and screaming, their formations shattered.

Reva watched from the edge of the blast radius, her golden eyes wide with horror. She had been riding to reinforce the capital. She had been riding to save her people. She had just lost half her command in the span of a heartbeat.

<div class="dialogue-block">
<p class="speech-line">"SCRAMBLE TO THE WALL! GET TO THE WALL! DO NOT STAY IN THE OPEN!"</p>
</div>

Her voice was raw, hoarse, barely audible over the chaos. The surviving Wohs surged toward the gate, their great legs churning the sand, their riders clinging to their backs. But the purple orb was still there. It was still growing. It rose into the air above the shattered formation, its violet light intensifying, its tendrils stretching outward like the arms of a hungry god. It grew larger than the orb that had struck the wall—larger, brighter, more terrible—and the sand below it began to scorch, turning from gold to black, from black to a deep, bruised shade of violet.

The ominous glow bathed the desert, painting the battlefield in shades of sickness and decay. The Truce was over. The Plague was still spreading. And the second orb was only the beginning.
